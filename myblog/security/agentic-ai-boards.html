Why GRC has to change

GRC today is a screenshot economy. The work product is real, the assurance is thin, and the cost curve is going the wrong way. Every problem below is a staffing problem dressed up as a process problem — which is exactly the shape of problem agents are good at.

Scale figures are planning estimates for a mid-size regulated organisation, not published benchmarks. Calibrate them against your own last audit cycle.

The forcing function is not efficiency. It is that your organisation is already shipping AI systems faster than your control framework can describe them — and a GRC function running on quarterly evidence cycles cannot govern a system that changes weekly.

Reimagining GRC — eight shifts

Not eight tools. Eight changes in where the work sits, what counts as proof, and who is accountable for the judgement.

Read the middle column as the argument. The left is what most programmes do today and the right is what the same work looks like once evidence is emitted rather than collected. The eight rows between them are the only changes that matter — and the band underneath is why any of it is worth doing.

The eight shifts, with the reasoning

How we make the GRC world a better place

Give people back their judgement. The best risk professionals spend most of their week on work that a script should do. Every hour returned to adjudication, control design and honest conversation with engineering is an hour that actually reduces risk.

Make assurance mean something again. A control that passes a test written to be passable protects nobody. Report whether the control would have caught the incident, and let that be the number the board sees.

Make the audit trail the product. Speed impresses nobody who matters. Provenance does. If any conclusion can be replayed against the evidence that existed when it was reached, the programme is defensible under scrutiny — and that is the only durable asset GRC builds.

Make it cheap to be honest. Most concealment in GRC is economic: raising a finding creates weeks of work for the person who raised it. Automate the cost of honesty down to near zero and the true state of the organisation surfaces on its own.

→The cycle, left to right — quarterly control testing

Read it as a sandwich. Policy sits above the work and is enforced at every call. Evidence sits below it and is written continuously, not assembled at the end. The agents run left to right in the middle, and the orchestrator is the only thing that sees the whole width — which is why the approval gates belong to it. Humans appear three times: to open the cycle, to adjudicate exceptions, and to sign. Everything else is machinery.

Build it in this order

Nine phases. Each one is useful on its own, so the programme survives a budget freeze at any point — which is the only realistic test of a cost-effective plan. The ordering is deliberate: the cheapest, least risky work comes first and earns the credibility that funds the rest.

01PHASE

Pick one cycle. Not a platform.

One control-testing cycle, end to end, in one domain. Everything after this reuses it.

  • Choose the cycle with the highest evidence volume and the lowest political heat — access reviews or change management, usually.
  • Write down what the cycle costs you today in analyst-days. That number is your entire business case; you will never have a cleaner baseline.
  • Define done as: this quarter's cycle completes with machine-collected evidence and a human signature.
Do not buy a platform yet. Anything you buy before you have run one cycle is bought against a guess. The first phase should cost you time, not licence fees.
Why not start broad?

Breadth is what kills these programmes. Six half-automated cycles produce no defensible assurance and no credible number; one complete cycle produces both, and it is the artifact you take to the board for funding.

02PHASE

Build the spine before any agent.

Obligations register, control library, and the crosswalk between them.

  • Every obligation gets a stable identifier. Every control traces to at least one obligation; orphan controls are cost with no assurance value.
  • Map controls to the frameworks that actually apply to you — and record which framework imposes which obligation, so you can test once and report many.
  • Name an owner per control. No owner, no control.
  • Version the mapping and treat a mapping change as an approved change.
This phase is data work, not AI work, and it is where the leverage is. It needs a spreadsheet, a fortnight and someone senior enough to arbitrate. Skipping it is the single most expensive mistake available to you.
How good does the spine need to be?

Good enough to answer: which obligations does this control satisfy, and who owns it. Perfection is not required; completeness for the chosen cycle is.

03PHASE

Connect the five systems that hold most of the evidence.

Read-only API access to the sources that cover roughly two-thirds of evidence requests.

  • Identity provider, cloud control plane, ticketing, HR system, source control. In most organisations these five carry the majority of evidence.
  • Service identity per source, read-only, scoped, short-lived. Never collect through a human's session — it inherits their privileges and breaks segregation of duties.
  • Hash and timestamp at the point of collection. Store the pointer, not the payload.
  • Validate freshness and completeness on ingest, and fail closed.
Buy connectors, build the thin layer. Writing and maintaining your own integrations is the most reliably underestimated cost in this entire programme.
What about the long tail?

Leave it manual. The last 30% of sources will consume 70% of the integration budget and cover controls you test annually. Automate them only once the core is stable.

04PHASE

One agent, one job: collect and normalise.

Evidence arrives assembled, validated and attributed — with no model in the decision path.

  • Deploy collection and normalisation first. It is the highest-drudgery, lowest-risk work in the cycle.
  • No orchestrator at this stage. One agent with a queue is not a distributed system and should not be built like one.
  • Deduplicate and normalise into a consistent shape so downstream testing is deterministic.
  • Ship it to the analysts and let them keep doing the judgement by hand for a full cycle.
This phase alone typically retires the majority of the clerical load. Most of the value, almost none of the risk. If budget stops here, you have still won.
Why hold the model back?

Because trust is the constraint, not capability. Give the organisation a quarter of watching evidence arrive correctly before you let anything draw a conclusion from it.

05PHASE

Add testing — code decides, the model explains.

Deterministic checks executed in code; the model classifies exceptions and drafts the narrative.

  • Write the test procedures as code against the normalised evidence. Never let a language model do the arithmetic or the pass/fail.
  • Use the model for what it is good at: classifying exceptions, summarising, drafting the finding, spotting the pattern across quarters.
  • Procedures come from an approved library. The agent does not invent a test at runtime.
  • Route every exception to a human queue. Approve nothing automatically yet.
Use the smallest model that passes your eval for classification, and reserve the large one for exception narrative. Model choice is the difference between pennies and pounds per control at volume.
How do I know the model is good enough?

Run it silently alongside the analysts for one cycle and measure agreement. You want the disagreements — they tell you where the procedure is ambiguous, which is a finding in itself.

06PHASE

Introduce the orchestrator — only now.

A single decision point once you have three or more agents with real dependencies.

  • Trigger it on the risk calendar and on material change events, not just quarter end.
  • Least-context assembly, not maximum-context. Over-sharing between agents is the quiet data leak in these architectures.
  • Enforce policy at call time through one engine. Policy logic embedded per agent drifts within a quarter.
  • Log the plan. The plan is an audit artifact; retain it and record deviations.
An orchestrator before three agents is architecture as procrastination. It adds latency, cost and a new failure mode to a problem you do not have yet.
Which cooperation model?

Orchestrator-mediated, with a shared knowledge graph for state. Avoid peer-to-peer entirely until you have an audit trail you would defend in front of a regulator.

07PHASE

Wire the human gates and bind the attestation.

Approval that captures what the human actually saw, not that they clicked.

  • Define gate triggers in policy, by risk tier — not in prompt text, so they can be tested and evidenced.
  • Auto-attestation for low risk only, with the tier defined in writing and approved.
  • The signature binds to the evidence set exactly as it stood at signing. This is the whole point.
  • No agent closes its own finding. Closure is evidence-based re-test, never owner assertion.
  • Document the kill switch: how an agent is stopped, credentials revoked, in-flight actions rolled back. Then test it.
This phase adds almost no infrastructure cost and carries most of the defensibility. It is also the part that gets skipped under delivery pressure.
How many gates is too many?

Trend your override rate. If humans approve everything without change, the gate is theatre and should be re-tiered. If they override often, your policy or your model is wrong.

08PHASE

Reporting: one evidence set, three views.

Board, executive and owner views generated from the same records, so they cannot diverge.

  • Generate on demand rather than assembling monthly. Assembly is the cost you are removing.
  • Every number drills through to the evidence behind it. A metric that cannot be traced is a claim, not an assertion.
  • Publish the definition and the denominator alongside the metric, and version them.
  • Give the auditor self-serve read access. Their evidence re-requests are a metric in their own right.
Reuse the same evidence store for every audience. Separate reporting stacks per committee is how organisations end up defending two contradictory numbers in the same week.
What about the auditors?

Bring them in during this phase, not after. An auditor who has agreed the evidence model in advance is a collaborator; one who meets it at fieldwork is a risk.

09PHASE

Extend to vendor risk and vulnerability management.

The same spine, pointed at the two domains where questionnaires and severity scores have failed.

  • Vendors: continuous outside-in signal, contractual flow-down, a live subprocessor register to fourth party. Re-assess on event — breach, acquisition, a new model provider — not on calendar.
  • Vulnerabilities: correlate a live SBOM to advisories continuously and rank by reachability and field exposure rather than CVSS.
  • For fielded medical devices, the disposition decision and the regulatory judgement stay human. The analysis does not.
  • Register every AI system with an owner and an intended use. Pin model and prompt versions and re-baseline tests on upgrade.
These reuse the connectors, the evidence store and the audit trail you already built. Marginal cost, disproportionate visibility — which is why they come last, not first.
Why last?

Because they are the two domains where executives most want to start and where an immature spine fails most publicly. Earn the credibility on control testing first.

What “easy” actually means

Easy is not a nicer interface. Easy means the person does less — fewer places to go, fewer times they are asked, fewer decisions to make, and nothing re-keyed. If your system does more but the control owner still gets three emails, you have not made anything easier. You have moved the work and kept the ask.

Someone in engineering or operations who owns four controls and thinks about them twice a year.

The control owner

No new system to log into. Asked once, not three times by three teams. The request arrives with the context already attached, a real deadline, and a one-click way to confirm or dispute.

Test: They never have to ask what you need or where to put it.
The person currently spending most of the week reformatting exports.

The GRC analyst

Opens a queue of exceptions, not a folder of screenshots. Every item arrives with the evidence, the history and a draft conclusion to accept, amend or reject.

Test: Their day is decisions, not retrieval.
Internal or external, on a deadline, sceptical by default.

The auditor

Self-serve read access to evidence with provenance intact. No sample requests by email, no waiting on a coordinator, no version confusion.

Test: They can answer their own question without asking you.
Signing something that carries their name.

The executive

One number per question, and every number drills to the records behind it. The definition is published next to it.

Test: They can defend it in the room without a pre-brief.

The five properties of easy

  • Asked once. One request per person per cycle, aggregated across every framework that needs it.
  • Context attached. The request carries what it is for, what good looks like, and the deadline.
  • Right by default. The pre-filled answer is correct often enough that confirming is the normal path.
  • Reversible. Anything a person does can be undone without a support ticket, so the safe move is to act.
  • No new home. It arrives where they already work — the ticket queue, the chat channel, the inbox.

Three audiences, three questions, one evidence set. The board asks whether the organisation is protected. The C-suite asks whether the function is efficient. Committees ask whether the mechanism is working. Percentage of controls tested answers none of those, which is why it should not be the headline anywhere.

Metric hygiene — non-negotiable

  • Every number drills to evidence. If it cannot be traced to the records behind it, it is a claim, not an assertion.
  • Publish the denominator. Most metric disputes are denominator disputes discovered in the meeting.
  • Version the definition. A metric whose definition changed silently is worse than no metric.
  • Trend, never point. A single quarter tells the board nothing they can act on.
  • Name the owner, not the team. Accountability that lands on a function lands on nobody.
  • Report the uncomfortable one. Attestations signed without evidence, and closures that failed re-test. If your pack has no bad news, nobody in the room believes the good news.

Why product security has to change

Product security is the one function that has already lost the volume argument. Every scanner you add widens the gap between what is found and what anyone can act on, and the gap is where the credibility goes. These are the nine places it hurts.

Scale figures are planning estimates for a mid-size product organisation, not published benchmarks. Calibrate against your own last quarter.

The forcing function is not the CVE count. It is that AI is now writing your code, entering your dependencies and shipping inside your products — three new attack surfaces arriving at once, into a review process that was already the bottleneck.

The eight shifts, with the reasoning

How we make product security a better place to work

Give engineers a queue they trust. Credibility is the scarce resource in this function, and noise is exactly how it gets spent. A short accurate queue changes the relationship with engineering more than any platform will.

Turn seniors from authors into reviewers. Your best security engineers should be challenging designs, not writing the first eighty percent of a threat model that a machine can draft.

Answer the exposure question in minutes. Which fielded versions contain this component and which customers are affected — without asking a human to go and look. Everything about incident quality follows from that one capability.

Automate the work, never the accountability. Someone signs, and can show exactly what they saw when they signed it. That is the line that keeps this defensible in front of a regulator.

Humans appear four times: to challenge the design, to accept the fix, to make the release decision, and to sign the advisory. Everything else is machinery.

Build it in this order

Eight phases. The ordering is chosen so that the cheapest, least risky work comes first and buys the credibility with engineering that funds the rest. In product security, credibility is the constraint - not budget, and not model capability.

01PHASE

Instrument before you automate.

One number you trust: what security work actually costs your engineers today.

  • Measure findings reaching developers per sprint, the share dismissed as invalid, and hours lost to security friction per release.
  • Pick one product line and one pipeline. Not the whole estate, and not the most political one.
  • Define done as: this quarter, a developer receives a materially shorter and more accurate queue.
Costs you two weeks and no licence fees. Every later business case rests on this baseline, and it is impossible to reconstruct once you have started changing things.
Why not start with a tool?

Because you will not be able to prove it worked. Teams that skip the baseline end up arguing about whether things improved, which is an argument security always loses.

02PHASE

Fix the signal before adding intelligence.

Correlated, deduplicated findings from the tools you already own.

  • Normalise output from SAST, SCA, secrets and cloud scanning into one shape with one identifier per real issue.
  • Deduplicate across tools. The same issue reported by three scanners is one finding.
  • Suppress with an expiry and a reason, never permanently and never silently.
  • Route by code ownership so findings land on a person, not a queue.
This phase uses no AI at all and typically removes the majority of the noise. It is the cheapest credibility you will ever buy with an engineering organisation.
Is this really phase two?

Yes. Pointing a model at an incoherent finding stream produces confident summaries of noise, and you only get one first impression with developers.

03PHASE

Build the security knowledge graph.

Code, assets, findings, SBOM, incidents and owners, joined and queryable.

  • Join repositories to deployed assets to fielded product versions. This join is what answers the exposure question.
  • Attach ownership, and keep it current from the source of truth rather than a spreadsheet.
  • Record decisions - accepted, deferred, fixed - with the evidence that was in view at the time.
  • Make the SBOM live across every supported version, not generated at release.
This is data engineering, not AI, and it is the highest-leverage work in the programme. Every agent you deploy afterwards is only as good as this graph.
How complete does it need to be?

Complete enough to answer: which fielded versions contain this component, and who owns the fix. If you can answer that in minutes, the graph is good enough to build on.

04PHASE

First agent: triage and enrichment.

Findings arrive reachability-ranked, deduplicated and enriched, with no model in the decision path.

  • Automate exploitability and reachability analysis. Rank by whether the vulnerable function is invoked and whether the asset is exposed.
  • Dismiss the unreachable automatically, with the reasoning recorded and reversible.
  • Do not let a model decide severity. Let it explain, summarise and draft.
  • Run it silently beside your engineers for one cycle and measure agreement before it becomes the queue.
Highest drudgery, lowest risk, and it lands where developers feel it. If the budget stops here, you have still won the credibility you need for everything else.
What if the agent is wrong?

Every dismissal is reversible and logged, and you review a sample every cycle. Measure the disagreements; they tell you where your rules are ambiguous.

05PHASE

Put guidance in the developer's flow.

The secure path becomes the fast path, in the pull request and the IDE.

  • Comment on pull requests only where precision is high. Noise here costs you the developers permanently.
  • Ship fixes as diffs the developer can accept, with the test that proves the fix.
  • Surface the relevant standard and the relevant precedent inline, so nobody has to go and find it.
  • Track acceptance rate of your suggestions as the product metric it is.
Adoption is the whole game. A tool developers want is worth three they tolerate, and acceptance rate tells you which one you have built.
How do I avoid becoming noise?

Set a precision floor and hold to it. Comment on fewer things than you could; the restraint is what makes the comments read.

06PHASE

Threat modelling and design review, drafted by machine.

Every feature gets a model. Senior engineers review rather than author.

  • Draft from design artifacts, data flows and the repository itself.
  • Generate abuse cases exhaustively; that is where models genuinely outperform a tired human on a Friday.
  • Re-run on architectural change so the model tracks the system.
  • The senior engineer prunes, challenges and signs. That signature is the control.
Turns the scarcest resource you have - senior security judgement - from author into reviewer. The multiplier is on people, not licences.
Will the drafts be any good?

Good enough to argue with, which is the point. The value is that a review now happens at all for the two hundred features that previously got none.

07PHASE

Introduce the orchestrator - only now.

One decision point once you have three or more agents with real dependencies.

  • Least-context routing. Source code and secrets are the two things never to over-share between agents.
  • One policy engine consulted at call time by every agent.
  • Per-agent identity, scoped and short-lived. Never a shared service account.
  • Log the plan, the tool calls, the model version and the prompt version on every run.
An orchestrator before three agents is architecture as procrastination. Build it when the dependencies are real, not when the diagram looks better.
Which cooperation model?

Orchestrator-mediated with a shared graph for state. Peer-to-peer only inside one trust boundary, with mirrored logging.

08PHASE

Extend to release gates, PSIRT and the fielded fleet.

The same graph, pointed at the decisions that carry regulatory weight.

  • Verify attestations and provenance at the gate rather than recording and trusting them.
  • Automate fleet exposure analysis: which fielded versions contain the component, which customers are affected.
  • Draft advisories and customer notifications from the finding record; a human owns wording and timing.
  • For regulated products, the disposition and the regulatory judgement stay human. The analysis does not.
Reuses everything already built. Marginal cost, disproportionate regulatory value - and it is what a 524B submission or an FDA inspection actually asks you to demonstrate.
Why last?

Because it is the most visible and least forgiving place to be wrong. Earn the reliability on triage first.

What “easy” actually means

Easy is not a nicer dashboard. Easy means the person does less — fewer places to go, fewer things to disprove, nothing re-keyed. In product security there is a sharper test than in most domains: if a developer has to open your tool at all, you have already lost most of the benefit.

Ships features, thinks about security when something blocks them.

The developer

One queue, short, and everything in it is real. Findings arrive in the pull request with the reachable path and a fix they can accept. Nothing asks them to open a security tool.

Test: They never have to prove a finding is wrong.
Currently triaging scanner output instead of doing security engineering.

The product security engineer

Opens a queue of decisions, not a queue of alerts. Threat models arrive drafted. Precedent from past incidents surfaces at triage automatically.

Test: Their week is judgement, not retrieval.
Accountable for shipping on a date.

The release manager

Gates run in minutes, explain exactly what failed and what would clear it, and never block on something already accepted.

Test: They can predict whether the gate will pass before they run it.
Assessing whether the process is real.

The auditor or regulator

Self-serve evidence with provenance: which version, which component, which decision, who signed and what they saw.

Test: They can reconstruct any decision without an interview.

The five properties of easy

  • Comes to them. The pull request, the IDE, the pipeline — never a new console.
  • Everything in the queue is real. Precision above volume, always. One bad finding costs ten good ones.
  • Arrives with the fix. A diff to accept and the test that proves it, not a link to a CVE.
  • Explains the block. A gate that fails must say what failed and exactly what would clear it.
  • Reversible. Dismissals, suppressions and overrides can be undone, so acting is the safe move.

Metric hygiene — non-negotiable

  • Never report finding counts as an achievement. They measure scanner configuration, not security.
  • Every number drills to evidence. Which commit, which version, which decision, who signed.
  • Publish the denominator. Coverage claims collapse the moment someone asks what the base was.
  • Trend, never point. One quarter of escape rate tells the board nothing actionable.
  • Report the uncomfortable one. The unpatchable exposure register and the false positive rate. A pack with no bad news is a pack nobody believes.

Adding AI security testing to what you already run

Do not stand up a separate AI security programme. It will be under-resourced, ignored by engineering and duplicated within a year. Extend the four things you already have — the asset inventory, the threat model, the test suite and change control — and the AI work inherits the governance rather than needing its own.

ASTEP

Discover and register, before anything else

You cannot test what nobody has written down.

  • Sweep for inference endpoints, model artifacts, vector stores, prompt files and MCP server configurations across repos and cloud.
  • Register each with an owner, an intended use, the data it touches and the tools it can invoke.
  • Expect to find components nobody declared. That gap is itself the first finding, and it is the one that gets the programme funded.
Costs a fortnight of scripting and some uncomfortable conversations. Every other AI control depends on it.
What if teams resist declaring?

Make registration the path to something they want — approved model access, a faster gate, a shared prompt library. Enforcement alone drives shadow deployment, which is worse than the problem you started with.

BSTEP

Extend the threat model, do not fork it

Ten AI categories added to the model that already gets reviewed.

  • Add prompt injection, output handling, poisoning, disclosure, extraction, excessive agency, guardrail bypass, supply chain, resource exhaustion and version drift.
  • Draft them with the Threat Modeling Agent; a senior engineer prunes and signs, exactly as for everything else.
  • Re-run on architectural change — and treat a new tool grant to an agent as an architectural change.
No new process, no new meeting, no new document owner. Reuse is the point.
Is a generic list good enough?

As a floor, yes. The value comes from the second pass, where you ask which of these your product's specific data and tool access actually make exploitable.

CSTEP

Write the pass criteria as numbers, in advance

Thresholds agreed with the product owner before the first test runs.

  • Attack success rate per ingestion path, leakage rate per data class, guardrail suite pass rate, denied-invocation completeness.
  • State what blocks a release and what merely raises a finding. Ambiguity here is always resolved in favour of shipping.
  • Record the threshold and its approver. When it is renegotiated later, and it will be, the record is the control.
Free, and the single highest-value hour in this whole section. A test with no threshold is a demonstration.
Where do the first numbers come from?

Your own first run. Measure, agree a floor no worse than that, then ratchet. Borrowed industry thresholds will not survive their first argument with a release date.

DSTEP

Three tiers, three cadences

Fast tests in CI, slow tests nightly, humans per release.

  • CI: deterministic guardrail assertions and output-sink rejection tests. Seconds, not minutes, or developers will route around them.
  • Nightly: the full adversarial corpus across every ingestion path, with results diffed against the last baseline.
  • Per release: human red team on the novel surface. Creativity is the part that does not automate, so spend it where the design is new.
Tier one is cheap and runs constantly; tier two is the one to watch, since adversarial suites at scale get expensive. Cap the nightly spend and measure cost per run.
How big should the corpus be?

Start with a few hundred payloads covering each ingestion path, and grow it from your own findings. Every bypass anyone discovers against your product becomes a permanent regression test.

ESTEP

Change control and runtime monitoring

The suite re-runs on every version change; production reports the same categories.

  • Pin model, prompt and dataset versions. Any change opens a ticket and triggers a re-baseline.
  • Monitor injection attempts detected, tool denials by tier, output validation failures and query-volume anomalies.
  • Feed production signal back into the corpus. Real attempts against your product are the most valuable test cases you will ever get.
Almost no infrastructure cost, and it is what converts point-in-time testing into continuing assurance you can show a regulator.
Why does this keep coming up?

Because silent version drift is the failure mode most likely to invalidate your evidence without anyone noticing. It deserves the repetition.

Legacy products: a different programme, not a smaller one

Legacy is where product security programmes quietly fail, because the modern playbook assumes a reproducible build, a live pipeline and field telemetry — and legacy has none of the three. Run it as its own programme with its own success criteria, or it will consume the main one and satisfy nobody.

FSTEP

Inventory from the artifact, not the build

A derived SBOM for every supported product you cannot rebuild.

  • Run binary composition analysis on the shipped image or firmware; accept the confidence levels it gives you and record them.
  • Join it to fielded-version records so a component maps to versions, and versions map to customers.
  • Mark derived inventories as derived. An SBOM with unstated confidence is worse than an honest gap.
A tool licence and a few weeks per product family. Nothing else in the legacy programme works without it.
How accurate is derived analysis?

Good at identifying well-known components, weaker on modified or statically-linked code. Sufficient to answer the exposure question at triage, which is the job it needs to do.

GSTEP

Rebuild the version-to-customer map once

Which customers are running which version, answerable as a query.

  • Consolidate service records, distributor data, installation logs and support tickets into one join. It is unglamorous and it is the whole capability.
  • Accept partial coverage and record the gaps explicitly rather than presenting an optimistic number.
  • Keep it current through the service process, not through periodic heroics.
Pure data work with no AI required. It converts every future incident from a research project into a query.
Who owns this map?

Usually service or commercial operations, not security — which is exactly why it needs an executive sponsor. Security is a consumer of this data, not its owner.

HSTEP

Ship guidance when you cannot ship code

Compensating controls as a versioned, published deliverable.

  • Segmentation, hardening, monitoring guidance and operational restrictions, written for the person who actually operates the device.
  • Capture the tribal mitigations from the engineers who still remember, before they leave. This is perishable knowledge.
  • Version and publish it as you would a patch, with a clear statement of what it does and does not mitigate.
Cheap to produce, disproportionately valued by customers and regulators. It is often the only honest answer available.
Does guidance satisfy a regulator?

Documented, communicated and justified mitigation for a product that cannot be patched is a recognised outcome. Silence is not. The failure mode regulators penalise is the absence of a decision, not the absence of a patch.

ISTEP

Publish end-of-support and hold an exposure register

Everything you are knowingly carrying, with a name against it.

  • State when security support ends, per product. Ambiguity transfers risk to you indefinitely.
  • Register every reachable vulnerability with no available fix: why, what mitigates it, who accepted it, when the acceptance expires.
  • Re-present expiring acceptances to the original approver. Nothing becomes permanent by default.
  • Report the register to the board. It is the most uncomfortable slide in the pack and the one that proves the rest is honest.
Costs nothing but candour. It is the difference between managed legacy risk and undisclosed legacy risk, which is the distinction that matters after an incident.
Won't this alarm the board?

It should, proportionately. A register that surprises them later is far more damaging than one that informs them now, and the first version is always the worst one you will ever show.

Metric additions for these two areas

  • AI attack success rate per ingestion path, trended — the headline AI number for the board.
  • Registered AI components under current threat model over all discovered components.
  • Re-baseline compliance: model, prompt and dataset changes that triggered a full re-test, over all changes detected.
  • Derived SBOM coverage: supported legacy products with a current derived inventory, over all supported legacy products.
  • Unpatchable reachable exposure: count, installed base affected, and the accountable executive named against each.
  • Compensating control currency: legacy products whose published guidance has been reviewed within the year.

Why the SOC has to change

The SOC is the one function whose workload is set entirely by other people — attackers, vendors, and whoever last wrote a noisy detection. Everything below follows from that: a queue sized by what the tooling emits rather than by what the team can absorb.

Scale figures are planning estimates for a mid-size enterprise SOC, not published benchmarks. Calibrate against your own last quarter.

The forcing function is speed. Identity and cloud intrusions complete in minutes while approval chains run in hours — so the question is no longer whether to grant machines authority, but which reversible actions to grant, inside what blast radius, written down in advance.

Reimagining the SOC — eight shifts

Not eight tools. Eight changes in what reaches an analyst, and what that analyst is asked to do with it.

Read the middle column as the argument. The left is how most SOCs run today; the right is the same work once enrichment happens before human contact. The band underneath is why it matters beyond efficiency.

The eight shifts, with the reasoning

How we make the SOC a better place to work

Give analysts cases, not alerts. The first action of a shift should be a decision, not a search. Everything else about morale, retention and quality follows from that one change.

Make every detection a product. An owner, a stated hypothesis, a test that proves it fires, and a review date. Unowned detections are the source of most noise, and noise is what makes the job unbearable.

Prove coverage rather than asserting it. Run the technique and check the detection fires end to end. A green heat map built from a spreadsheet is a comfort, not a control.

Automate the hands, never the authority. Reversible action by written policy, destructive action by a person, and every automated step with a tested undo. That line is what makes machine-speed response defensible.

→The cycle, left to right — signal to learning

Read it as a sandwich with a loop. The policy and control plane sits above the work; the incident graph sits below it and is written continuously. Agents run left to right, and the orchestrator is the only component that sees the whole width. The analyst appears three times — to choose the hypothesis, to approve above tier, and to sign the RCA. The dashed return is the part most SOCs never close: every finished case should improve the detection that opened it, or the loop is broken.

Build it in this order

Eight phases. Three of them involve no AI at all, and they are the three that make everything after them work. In a SOC the binding constraint is rarely model capability — it is data quality, an unmanaged detection set, and analyst trust, in that order.

01PHASE

Baseline the alert reality, honestly.

One page of numbers nobody in the SOC will dispute.

  • Alerts per analyst per shift, share auto-closed, share reopened, and the ten detections generating most of the volume.
  • Median dwell from first signal to containment on confirmed incidents, and how you actually know.
  • Time spent per investigation on lookups versus on thinking. Sample it for a week; the ratio will make the case for you.
Two weeks and no licence spend. Nothing later is provable without it, and after you start changing things the baseline is gone.
Won't the numbers be embarrassing?

Yes, and that is the point. A baseline nobody is uncomfortable with is a baseline nobody measured properly.

02PHASE

Fix the data before you add intelligence.

Normalised, enriched telemetry with asset and identity context attached at ingest.

  • Resolve every event to an asset and an identity at ingest. Doing it at investigation time is doing it thousands of times instead of once.
  • Normalise field names across sources so a query means one thing everywhere.
  • Fill the logging gaps that make your priority detections impossible, and record the ones you cannot fill.
  • Establish an incident graph schema now: users, hosts, processes, files, cloud assets, events, indicators, timeline.
Unglamorous data engineering. Every agent you deploy is only as good as this layer, and no model compensates for missing identity context.
Can't the agent do the joining?

It can, badly and repeatedly, at cost. Joining once at ingest is cheaper, faster and produces evidence with consistent provenance.

03PHASE

Kill the noise you already know about.

A queue the team could plausibly drain, before any AI is involved.

  • Take the top ten noisiest detections and fix, own or retire each one. This alone often halves the queue.
  • Give every remaining detection an owner and a stated hypothesis.
  • Expire every existing suppression and force a re-decision by a named person.
  • Deduplicate related alerts into single incidents at the source.
No AI required and it removes most of the volume. Pointing agents at an unmanaged rule set produces confident summaries of noise.
Is this really before the agents?

Yes. Automating triage on a broken detection set encodes the noise and makes it harder to see.

04PHASE

First agent: enrichment, with no authority.

Every alert arrives with the ten lookups already done and nothing auto-closed.

  • Identity context, endpoint context, cloud activity, intel matching, prior occurrences and asset criticality - assembled automatically.
  • Present it; do not conclude from it. The analyst still decides everything for now.
  • Run silently alongside the team for a cycle and measure whether analysts agree with the assembled picture.
  • Read-only service identities, per agent, scoped and logged.
Highest drudgery, zero blast radius, and analysts feel it immediately. If funding stops here you have still transformed tier-1.
Why no auto-close yet?

Because trust is the constraint. Let the team watch it be right for a quarter before it is allowed to decide anything.

05PHASE

Investigation agent and the incident graph.

Correlation, timeline and drafted hypotheses in one place.

  • Assemble the graph across identity, endpoint, cloud and network automatically on every escalated case.
  • Draft hypotheses and the queries that would confirm or eliminate each; the analyst chooses which to pursue.
  • Deploy a challenger agent arguing the opposite conclusion. It is cheap and it catches the confident error.
  • Capture queries, evidence and reasoning as structured state so the case is reproducible and reusable.
This is where investigation quality improves rather than just speeds up. The graph is also your institutional memory, which is the asset that compounds.
Does the challenger agent actually help?

Disproportionately. Most bad SOC calls are confident single-hypothesis calls, and a cheap second opinion is the correction.

06PHASE

Response with written action tiers.

Autonomy defined in policy, not in a prompt.

  • Read auto. Reversible containment auto within a defined blast radius. Disruptive by asset class and rehearsal. Destructive by a person.
  • Every automated action needs a tested undo, and the undo is tested before the action is enabled.
  • No agent closes its own incident.
  • Break-glass is loud, logged and reviewed every single time it is used.
Adds little infrastructure and carries most of the defensibility. It is also the phase most likely to be skipped under pressure.
How wide should the first tier be?

Narrower than feels necessary. Widen on measured agreement between agent recommendation and human decision, never on optimism.

07PHASE

Detection engineering as a pipeline.

Ideas move from intel to production with owners, tests and an age metric.

  • Intel or incident to hypothesis to telemetry check to detection to validation to production, with ageing at each stage.
  • Every detection ships with a test that proves it fires end to end, including routing and alerting.
  • Validate priority techniques continuously and alert on regression rather than running an annual purple team.
  • Report validated coverage separately from claimed coverage and treat the gap as the finding.
The highest-leverage workflow on the board and the one most often left as a backlog. Agents draft detections well; validation is what makes them real.
Where do the priority techniques come from?

Your own incidents and your threat model, not a generic top-twenty. Coverage of what actually targets you beats breadth.

08PHASE

Widen autonomy on evidence.

Each promotion into the automatic tier is a decision with data behind it.

  • Measure agreement between agent recommendation and analyst decision, by action type, for a full quarter.
  • Promote one action type at a time, and keep the rollback path tested.
  • Trend override rate. Never overriding means the gate is theatre; always overriding means the agent is wrong.
  • Re-baseline the whole suite whenever the model, the prompts or the detection set change materially.
Costs nothing but discipline, and it is what keeps this defensible as scope grows. Scope creep from an unreviewed pilot is the usual failure.
When is it enough?

When the remaining human decisions are the ones you would want a person making anyway. That point arrives sooner than most teams expect and later than most vendors claim.

What “easy” actually means

Easy is not a better console. Easy means the analyst does less — fewer lookups, fewer places, fewer things to reconstruct. The sharpest test in a SOC is the 3am one: could someone tired, alone and unsure of their authority make the right call in two minutes and defend it in the morning.

Working a queue at 3am.

The tier-1 analyst

Cases, not alerts. Every lookup already done, prior occurrences attached, and one screen with the decision on it. No console hopping to answer an obvious question.

Test: Their first action of a shift is a decision, not a search.
Picking up a live case mid-investigation.

The incident responder

The case state is the handoff: current hypotheses, what has been ruled out and on what evidence, what is pending, what is next.

Test: They can take over without asking anyone what happened.
Responsible for whether anything gets caught at all.

The detection engineer

A pipeline with owners and ageing, tests that prove a rule fires end to end, and continuous validation that alerts on regression.

Test: They find out a detection broke from the system, not from a missed incident.
Woken at 3am to authorise something.

The on-call manager

The tier is written down, so the question is a policy lookup rather than a judgement call, and the evidence is on one screen.

Test: They can decide in two minutes and defend it in the morning.

The five properties of easy

  • Lookups already done. Nothing that a script could have fetched should wait for a human to fetch it.
  • One screen, one decision. Evidence, precedent and the recommended action in the same place.
  • Precedent attached. The last three times this pattern appeared, and what it turned out to be.
  • Authority written down. At 3am the tier should be a lookup, not a judgement call about what you are allowed to do.
  • Reversible. Every automated action has a tested undo, so acting is the safe move rather than the brave one.

Metrics that survive the room

The board asks whether an attacker would be caught and stopped. The C-suite asks whether the function is efficient and sustainable. Committees ask whether the mechanism works. Alerts triaged and tickets closed answer none of those, and they improve when the team skims faster — which is precisely the behaviour you do not want to reward.

Why identity has to change

Identity is the control plane every other control depends on, and it is governed by a ritual that removes almost nothing. Access is granted forever, reviewed in bulk, described in codes nobody can read, and held increasingly by principals that are not people at all.

Scale descriptions are qualitative characterisations, not published benchmarks. Calibrate against your own last certification campaign.

The forcing function is that identity is now the primary attack path and non-human identities already outnumber human ones — and the governance model was designed for employees, annual campaigns and a directory you could read in an afternoon.

Reimagining identity — eight shifts

Not eight tools. Eight changes in how access is granted, kept, proven and removed.

Read the middle column as the argument. The left is how most identity programmes run today; the right is the same work once entitlements mean something and grants carry an expiry. The band underneath is why it matters beyond compliance.

The eight shifts, with the reasoning

How we make identity a better place to work

Make least privilege the easy path. Every shared account, borrowed session and over-broad request is a measurement of how slow you made the compliant route. Fix the speed and the behaviour follows without a campaign.

Make every entitlement explainable. Nobody can request, approve or revoke what they cannot read. Plain language is not a nicety here; it is the precondition for every other control on this board.

Govern non-human identity as first-class. Service accounts, workloads and your own agents — owned, expiring and reviewable. This is the fastest-growing and least governed population you have.

Automate the grant, never the accountability. Someone owns every entitlement, every exception and every expiry date, and the record shows what they saw when they decided.

→The identity lifecycle, left to right

Read it as a sandwich. The policy engine sits above the work and is evaluated at request time rather than at audit time; the IAM knowledge graph sits below it and is written at the moment of decision. The agents run left to right across the lifecycle, and the orchestrator is the only component that sees the full effective permission being granted — which is why the approval gates belong to it. Humans appear four times: to approve above tier, to authorise elevation, to certify with evidence, and to own the exception.

Build it in this order

Eight phases. The first three involve no AI at all and they are the ones that decide whether anything after them works. Identity is the domain where the data problem is the whole problem: an unresolved identity graph and an unreadable entitlement catalogue defeat any model you point at them.

01PHASE

Inventory every identity, including the non-human ones.

One list of principals, each with an owner.

  • Reconcile HR, directory, cloud and application sources. Entity resolution first - duplicates are how leavers keep access.
  • Include service accounts, workload identities, API keys, pipeline credentials and any agents you have already deployed.
  • Flag every principal with no named human owner. That list is your first finding and it will be longer than anyone expects.
Data work, a few weeks, no licence spend. Every later capability inherits the accuracy of this step, and no model compensates for an unresolved identity graph.
Why start with non-human?

Because they are excluded from every existing control and hold most of the standing privilege. It is the largest gap and the least contested to close.

02PHASE

Make entitlements mean something.

A catalogue a human can read, with an owner per entry.

  • Draft plain-language descriptions from configuration and observed usage; the owner confirms rather than authors.
  • Attach a risk rating, what it grants, and what it enables in combination.
  • Start with entitlements that appear in reviews or carry privilege. You do not need all forty thousand on day one.
The unglamorous prerequisite. Reviews, SoD, least privilege and risk scoring all depend on meaning, and none of them work without it.
Can the descriptions be trusted?

Draft by machine, confirmed by the owner. Unconfirmed descriptions are a hypothesis, and should be labelled as one in the review.

03PHASE

Fix leaver and mover before anything clever.

Revocation that reaches every system and confirms.

  • Fan revocation out to every connected system and require positive confirmation from each; silence raises an exception.
  • Treat a transfer as a revocation event with a grant attached - compare new role profile against current holdings and propose the delta.
  • Reconcile continuously against HR data and report accounts with no matching active identity as a standing metric.
Cheap, high-signal, and it fixes the failure auditors and attackers both look for first. Revocation is the safest thing to automate in identity.
Why is mover the hard one?

Because it needs an answer to what the person no longer needs, which nobody owns. The delta comparison is what makes it tractable.

04PHASE

First agent: evaluate requests, do not grant them.

Every request arrives assessed, with a recommendation and its reasoning.

  • Evaluate policy, SoD, peer profile, prior usage and context in one pass, and surface the narrower alternative where one exists.
  • Recommend only. Humans still approve everything for a full cycle.
  • Measure agreement between recommendation and human decision by entitlement risk band.
  • Read-only service identities, scoped per agent and logged.
Highest drudgery, no blast radius, and approvers feel it immediately. If funding stops here, access requests already got better.
When can it auto-approve?

When agreement in a risk band has been high for a quarter, and the action is reversible. Promote one band at a time.

05PHASE

Reviews: from bulk campaign to micro-certification.

Small batches, triggered by change and risk, with evidence attached.

  • Five items at a time, not four hundred, presented with meaning, last-used date and peer comparison.
  • Revocation executes automatically and restoration is one click, so reviewers can afford to be honest.
  • Default to removal on non-response, with notice, an appeal path and exclusions for critical access.
  • Report revocation rate alongside completion rate, and never completion rate alone.
Turns a compliance ritual into a control that actually removes access. The metric change is half the intervention.
Won't auto-removal cause outages?

Rarely, if last-used data drives it and restoration is trivial. Pilot on a friendly population and publish the restoration numbers.

06PHASE

Attack standing privilege, starting with privileged access.

JIT elevation, expiry at approval, reclamation on non-use.

  • Elevate on demand with a reason, expire automatically, and make the elevation faster than the workaround.
  • Set an expiry date at the moment of approval on every grant, privileged or not.
  • Reclaim access unused for a defined period; notify first, remove second, restore trivially.
  • Right-size cloud permissions from observed usage, proposed as a pull request against the infrastructure code.
The highest risk-reduction phase on this list. Standing privileged access is a small population carrying most of the exposure.
Where does it stall?

On the elevation being slower than the shortcut. Optimise the elevation path obsessively before widening the policy.

07PHASE

SoD and policy as code, enforced at request time.

Toxic combinations prevented rather than discovered.

  • Move the matrix into policy as code, evaluated before fulfilment, with the conflicting entitlement named in the response.
  • Exceptions require a justification and an expiry, and are reported by volume and by approver.
  • Simulate any bulk policy change and show exactly who loses what before executing it.
  • Version the policy and treat a change as a change requiring approval - it silently alters who can do what.
Preventing a toxic combination costs a dialogue box. Removing one twelve months later costs a project and a negotiation with the business.
What about existing violations?

Baseline them, freeze new ones immediately, and burn the backlog down on a published schedule. Freezing first is what stops the problem growing.

08PHASE

  • Trend override rate: never overriding means the gate is theatre, always overriding means the model is wrong.
Costs discipline rather than money, and it is what keeps the programme defensible as scope grows. Scope creep from an unreviewed pilot is the usual failure.
What is the hard limit?

Anything that cannot be reversed quickly, and anything affecting more identities at once than you have rehearsed rolling back.

Why application security has to change

Detection was solved a decade ago. What was never solved is everything after the finding exists: who fixes it, with what, by when, and how anyone knows it worked. These eleven problems all live in that gap.

The eight shifts, with the reasoning

How we make application security a better place to work

Never open a ticket you could open a pull request for. A ticket transfers the work to someone who did not find the problem and does not know the fix. A pull request does the work. That single distinction is most of the value in this entire board.

Spend senior judgement on design, not diffs. Machines draft the threat model and the analysis; your scarcest people challenge and sign. The multiplier is on people rather than licences.

Hold a precision floor above everything else. Credibility with developers is the currency the programme runs on, and noise is exactly how it gets spent. Comment on fewer things than you could.

Automate the fix, never the accountability. Someone approves the merge, someone owns the exception, and the record shows what they saw when they decided.

→The cycle, left to right — discover to learn

The orchestrator scopes from the dependency graph, so a change is not a whole-estate scan — which is what keeps cost and latency survivable. The developer appears three times: to challenge the design, to accept the fix, and to own the exception. The dashed return is the part most programmes never close.

Build it in this order

Eight phases. The first three involve no AI at all. In application security the constraint has never been detection — it is that findings arrive faster than anyone can act on them, and that the people who must act are not the people who found them.

01PHASE

Baseline what developers actually receive.

One page: volume, accuracy, and how long a fix takes.

  • Findings reaching a developer per sprint, share dismissed as invalid, and hours per finding to resolve.
  • The ten rules generating most of the volume, and the share of the estate under any automated checking at all.
  • Pick one product line and one pipeline. Not the whole estate, and not the most political team.
Two weeks, no licence spend. Every later argument rests on this baseline and it cannot be reconstructed afterwards.
Why measure invalid rate first?

Because it predicts adoption better than anything else. A programme with a high invalid rate is spending credibility it will need later.

02PHASE

One pipeline of record.

Normalised, deduplicated findings from the tools you already own.

  • Normalise SAST, SCA, secrets, IaC and container output into one shape with one identifier per real issue.
  • Deduplicate across tools - the same issue from three scanners is one finding, one ticket, one owner.
  • Calibrate your own severity scale on your own exploitability data; vendor severity becomes an input.
  • Route by code ownership so findings land on a person rather than a queue.
No AI, and it removes most of the confusion. Pointing agents at an incoherent finding stream produces confident summaries of noise.
Do we need a new platform?

Usually not. This is a normalisation layer over the tools you have, and building it first tells you what a platform would need to do.

03PHASE

Tier the applications and attach business context.

A small tiering model with named owners.

  • Three axes - data sensitivity, exposure, business criticality - and few tiers. Nine dimensions will not be maintained.
  • Derive what you can from the graph: internet exposure, data classification, authentication reach.
  • Publish it and let teams challenge their tier. A disputed tier is a useful conversation.
  • Vary the gate, the review depth and the SLA by tier.
Cheap, and it is what stops a single global policy being calibrated for nothing. Business context beats CVSS every time.
Who owns the tier?

The application owner, with security as reviewer. Security-assigned tiers get argued with; owner-assigned ones get defended.

04PHASE

First agent: triage and reachability, no fixes yet.

Findings arrive ranked by whether they can actually be reached.

  • Automate exploitability and reachability analysis; dismiss the unreachable with the reasoning recorded and reversible.
  • Correlate against prior findings and the exception ledger so nothing accepted last month is re-raised.
  • Run it silently beside the team for a cycle and measure agreement before it becomes the queue.
  • Read-only service identities, scoped per agent, every tool call logged.
Highest drudgery, no blast radius. If funding stops here the queue is already survivable, which is most of the battle.
What about the dismissals?

Sampled and reviewed every cycle, and reversible. The disagreements tell you where your rules are ambiguous, which is itself a finding.

05PHASE

Remediation agent: ship diffs, not tickets.

Fixes arrive as pull requests with the test that proves them.

  • Auto-fix in tiers: dependency bumps and configuration first; logic changes proposed but never merged unreviewed.
  • Batch the boring ones - one pull request for forty identical updates gets merged; forty tickets do not.
  • Search for the same pattern elsewhere and open linked findings. One finding is usually a class.
  • Track fix acceptance rate as the primary product metric.
This is the phase that determines whether anything actually gets fixed. A ticket transfers work; a pull request does it.
How do we avoid a flood of bad pull requests?

Hold a precision floor and start with the highest-confidence categories. Ten merged diffs beat a hundred ignored ones.

06PHASE

Threat modelling drafted for every feature.

Senior engineers review rather than author.

  • Draft from design artifacts, data flows and the repository itself; generate abuse cases exhaustively.
  • Re-run on architectural change, and treat a new external integration as an architectural change.
  • The senior engineer prunes, challenges and signs. That signature is the control.
  • Feed the resulting requirements back as testable acceptance criteria.
Turns the scarcest resource you have from author into reviewer. The multiplier is on people, not licences.
Will the drafts be good enough?

Good enough to argue with, which is the point. The win is that a review happens at all for the features that previously got none.

07PHASE

Guardrails: policy as code, gates that explain themselves.

Fast, tiered, and always with a stated path through.

  • Policy in code, versioned and tested, evaluated in the pull request rather than at the end.
  • Every gate failure states exactly what failed and what would clear it.
  • Tier the gate by application tier and by reversibility of the action.
  • Cap the blast radius of any agent run and require approval beyond it.
Adds little cost and carries most of the defensibility. It is also the phase most likely to be skipped under delivery pressure.
How strict should the gate be?

Strict where the tier justifies it and advisory everywhere else. A uniformly strict gate is a uniformly ignored one.

08PHASE

Verification and the exception ledger.

Closed means re-tested; accepted means recorded, owned and expiring.

  • Closure requires a passing automated re-test against the original finding.
  • Every exception carries a justification, an owner, a tier-appropriate approver and an expiry.
  • Report first-time re-test pass rate and the ledger's volume and ageing to engineering leadership.
  • Make the ledger searchable at triage so nothing formally accepted is ever re-raised.
Costs almost nothing and closes the two gaps auditors and attackers both find first. Assertion-based closure is why backlogs look better than they are.
Why last?

Because it only works once the pipeline of record and the tiering exist. Built earlier it becomes another spreadsheet.

Metric hygiene — non-negotiable

  • Never report misconfiguration counts as an achievement. They move when a benchmark changes, not when risk does.
  • Report rollback events openly. Zero rollbacks usually means the automation tier is too narrow to matter.
  • Separate ephemeral from persistent coverage. Blending them hides exactly the gap that matters.
  • Trend, never point. A single quarter of cloud posture reflects what the pipeline deployed that week.
  • Report the uncomfortable one. The accepted risk ledger and the unowned resource share. A cloud pack with no bad news is a pack nobody is reading.
