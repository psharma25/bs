01

What is the EU Cyber Resilience Act?

One EU-wide cybersecurity law for products with digital elements, enforced through CE marking.

The Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) is Regulation (EU) 2024/2847. It sets mandatory cybersecurity requirements for hardware and software placed on the EU market, and it holds the manufacturer responsible across the whole product lifecycle, not just at launch.

It works like EU product safety law. A product must meet essential requirements, the manufacturer must document and assess conformity, affix the CE marking, and issue an EU Declaration of Conformity. Market surveillance authorities can then challenge products that fall short.

The four obligation blocks

Tap a box for detail

Product properties

Annex I, Part I. Secure by design and secure by default, with no known exploitable vulnerabilities at release.

Detail

Protection of confidentiality and integrity, access control and authentication, attack surface minimization, secure update mechanisms, resilience against denial of service, and security logging.

Vulnerability handling

Annex I, Part II. Running processes, not one-time artifacts.

Detail

Maintain a software bill of materials (SBOM), remediate without delay, run a coordinated vulnerability disclosure policy, test regularly, publish advisories, and distribute security updates free of charge.

Support period

Article 13. A declared period reflecting expected product use.

Detail

The support period must be at least five years, unless the product is expected to be in use for less time, in which case it matches the expected use. Where technically feasible, the product should tell users when support ends.

Evidence and marking

Technical documentation, conformity assessment, DoC, CE marking.

Detail

Technical documentation under Annex VII, conformity assessment under Annex VIII, the EU Declaration of Conformity, the CE marking, and clear user information and instructions.

Why it matters

The CRA turns product security into a market access condition. A product that cannot demonstrate conformity cannot legally be placed on the EU market. It regulates products, so it sits alongside NIS2 rather than replacing it.

02

Who does it apply to?

Every economic operator that brings a product with digital elements to the EU market.

Location does not matter. A manufacturer in the United States, India, or Japan carries the same obligations as one in Germany once its product reaches EU customers.

Roles and duties

Tap a role for detail

Manufacturers

Develop or manufacture a product and market it under their own name or trademark.

Duties

Carry the bulk of the obligations: essential requirements, conformity assessment, technical documentation, CE marking, support period, vulnerability handling, and reporting.

Importers

Established in the EU and place a non-EU manufacturer's product on the EU market.

Duties

Verify that conformity assessment was carried out, that technical documentation exists, and that CE marking and instructions are present. Must not place a product they know or should know is non-compliant.

Distributors

Make a product available on the EU market, other than the manufacturer or importer.

Duties

Check CE marking and required documentation, and act where they believe a product is non-compliant, including informing the manufacturer and authorities.

Authorized representatives

Appointed in writing by a manufacturer to act on its behalf in the EU.

Duties

Keep documentation available for authorities and cooperate with them. Non-EU manufacturers should appoint one before the reporting and market obligations bite.

Open-source stewards

Article 24. Foundations and similar bodies that sustain open-source projects used commercially.

Duties

A lighter regime: a documented cybersecurity policy and cooperation duties, with no CE marking. Their Article 24(3) reporting obligations apply from 11 December 2027.

Anyone who changes a product

Rebranding or substantially modifying a product pulls you into the manufacturer role.

Duties

A person who places a product under their own name or trademark, or makes a substantial modification to a product already on the market, takes on manufacturer obligations for that product.

Practical read

If you write software or build hardware that reaches EU customers directly or through a partner, assume you are a manufacturer until a documented analysis says otherwise. If you resell or integrate someone else's product, confirm in writing who holds the manufacturer role.

03

What products are in scope?

Products with digital elements, plus the remote data processing they depend on.

A product with digital elements is a software or hardware product, including its remote data processing solutions, whose intended purpose or reasonably foreseeable use includes a direct or indirect logical or physical data connection to a device or network. If your product contains software or firmware and connects to anything, treat it as in scope until a documented assessment shows otherwise.

Quick scope test

Examples

  • •Consumer IoT: speakers, cameras, wearables, appliances
  • •Industrial control systems and gateways
  • •Smart home devices and hubs
  • •Standalone software, apps, operating systems, firmware
  • •Components and microprocessors sold to other manufacturers

Typically in scope

  • ✓Hardware with network connectivity
  • ✓Embedded software and firmware
  • ✓Stand-alone software sold commercially
  • ✓Cloud-connected products, including the required cloud component
  • ✓Products with safety or security-critical functions

Typically out of scope

  • −Pure services such as SaaS with no product element
  • −Open source not placed on the market commercially
  • −Non-commercial or internal use only
  • −Products already regulated under sectoral legislation

Classification ladder: it sets your conformity route

Tap a tier

Default category

Self-assessment under Module A

The manufacturer assesses conformity internally, regardless of the technical specification used to demonstrate it. This covers most products on the market.

  • Examples: smart speakers, mobile apps, games, memory chips, most business software

What you still owe: full Annex I compliance, technical documentation, DoC, CE marking, and reporting. Self-assessment removes the third party, not the evidence.

Important, Annex III Class I

Self-assessment only if you fully apply the standards

A manufacturer may still self-assess, but only where it fully applies the relevant harmonized standards or a cybersecurity certification scheme. Without that, a notified body is required.

  • Examples: password managers, routers, network management systems, smart home hubs, identity management systems

Watch: harmonized standards are still publishing. Partial application does not qualify.

Important, Annex III Class II

Third-party assessment, no self-assessment route

A notified body must assess conformity regardless of whether you follow harmonized standards.

  • Examples: firewalls, intrusion detection and prevention systems, hypervisors, container runtimes, tamper-resistant microprocessors

Lead time: market estimates run from three to twelve months depending on complexity. Technical documentation must exist before the assessment starts.

Critical, Annex IV

The strictest route

These products face the strictest treatment and may be required to hold a European cybersecurity certificate under a relevant scheme once those schemes exist.

  • Examples: hardware security modules, smart meter gateways, smart cards and secure elements

Note: most manufacturers are not in this category. Confirm it rather than assume it.

Classification follows the product's core functionality, not every feature it happens to include. Where more than one category could apply, the stricter class applies. Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2392 gives technical descriptions of the important and critical categories.

Capacity risk

The rules on notified bodies applied from 11 June 2026, but designations were slow to appear in the Commission's NANDO database through mid-2026. Article 35(2) sets 11 December 2026 as the target for sufficient capacity. If you need a third-party route, start engagement early rather than wait for the list to fill.

04

What is out of scope?

Sector-specific products, non-commercial open source, pure services, and defense products.

Article 2 carves out products already governed by sectoral EU rules that address cybersecurity, along with a small set of other categories. Exclusion means the CRA does not apply, not that no cybersecurity obligation applies.

Excluded by Article 2

Medical devices and IVDs

Covered by Regulation (EU) 2017/745 and Regulation (EU) 2017/746. Health software outside the medical device regime can still fall under the CRA.

Motor vehicles

Covered by Regulation (EU) 2019/2144, extended by Delegated Regulation (EU) 2025/1535 to L-category vehicles under Regulation (EU) No 168/2013.

Civil aviation

Products certified under Regulation (EU) 2018/1139.

Marine equipment

Products covered by Directive 2014/90/EU.

Security and defense

Products developed or modified exclusively for national security or defense, or designed to process classified information.

Identical spare parts

Spare parts that replace identical components and are manufactured to the same specifications as the original.

Edge cases worth checking

Tap for detail

Free and open-source software

Outside a commercial activity, not covered. The test is broader than money changing hands.

Detail

Commercial activity can include paid support, freemium models, and monetization through advertising or personal data. A manufacturer that builds open-source code into a commercial product remains fully responsible for that product.

Software as a service

Pure SaaS is not a product with digital elements.

Detail

Where a remote data processing solution is required for the product to perform its functions, that solution is treated as part of the product and is in scope. A device that only works with its cloud pulls the cloud in with it.

Products already on the market

Different rules apply to the requirements and to reporting.

Detail

The essential requirements generally apply to products placed on the market after the application date, and to earlier products only where they undergo a substantial modification. Reporting reaches further: it applies to products already on the market from 11 September 2026.

Test and unfinished software

Limited-period testing releases are treated differently.

Detail

Software made available for a limited period for testing purposes, and clearly marked as such, is not treated as a product placed on the market.

Document the decision

Treat scope and classification as an auditable determination. Record the product, its core functionality, the annex categories considered, the conclusion, and the date. Market surveillance authorities can ask why you chose self-assessment.

05

Timeline: what to do by when

Seven stages from entry into force to ongoing obligations. Click any stage.

Key timeline and what to do

Tap a stage

10 December 2024

The CRA enters into force

Obligations do not bite yet, but the clock starts and the planning window opens.

  • Understand the regulation and what it changes for your products
  • Start a gap assessment against Annex I
  • Identify in-scope products
  • Establish governance and named ownership

11 June 2026

Conformity assessment provisions apply

Chapter IV on notified bodies applies, so Member States can designate bodies and list them in the NANDO database.

  • Determine product class: default, Annex III Class I or II, or Annex IV critical
  • Choose the conformity assessment route
  • Engage a notified body where one is required
  • Plan budget, lead time, and resourcing

Reality check: designations were slow to appear through mid-2026. Article 35(2) targets sufficient capacity by 11 December 2026.

11 September 2026 • in effect

Incident reporting obligations apply

Article 14 reporting is enforceable. ENISA's Single Reporting Platform went live the same day and is the only channel for these notifications.

  • Set up PSIRT processes and a named accountable owner
  • Register on the ENISA Single Reporting Platform and verify the account
  • Confirm the CSIRT designated as coordinator for your EU main establishment
  • Be ready for the 24 hour, 72 hour, and final report timelines
  • Test the incident reporting playbook before you need it

2026 to 2027

Implementation and readiness

The build phase. This is where most of the engineering effort sits.

  • Implement secure-by-design and secure-by-default configuration
  • Stand up vulnerability handling and coordinated disclosure
  • Produce technical documentation and risk assessments
  • Generate SBOMs from the build pipeline
  • Prepare user information and labeling, then validate and test
  • Close the gaps the assessment found

Before 11 December 2027

Final preparations

Evidence has to be complete before the date, not on it.

  • Complete the conformity assessment
  • Prepare the EU Declaration of Conformity
  • Affix the CE marking
  • Ensure all required documentation and evidence is in place
  • Train teams and brief distributors

11 December 2027

Full CRA application

The regulation applies in full.

  • Only compliant products may be placed on the EU market
  • Maintain security throughout the declared support period
  • Continue vulnerability management and reporting
  • Monitor and improve

Also from this date: Article 24(3) reporting obligations for open-source software stewards apply.

Post-2027

Ongoing obligations

The CRA is a lifecycle regulation, so the work does not end at CE marking.

  • Apply updates and patches through the support period
  • Report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents
  • Maintain documentation and advisories
  • Comply with support period commitments made to users
  • Track harmonized standards and guidance as they publish
Sequencing advice

Work backward from 11 December 2027. A Class II assessment can take several months, and technical documentation has to exist before the assessment starts. If you need a notified body, treat 2026 as the year to classify, scope, and engage.

06

Incident and vulnerability reporting

Live since 11 September 2026. One platform, three deadlines, two final report clocks.

What triggers a report

Reportable events

  • !Any actively exploited vulnerability in your product, once you become aware
  • !Any severe incident affecting the security of your product, once you become aware

Who reports

  • •Manufacturers, from 11 September 2026
  • •Open-source software stewards, from 11 December 2027

Who receives it

  • •The CSIRT designated as coordinator for your EU main establishment
  • •ENISA, in the same submission
  • •Impacted users, and where appropriate all users

The reporting clocks

Tap a deadline

Clock 1

Early warning, within 24 hours of becoming aware

A short notification that the event exists. You do not need root cause, exploit mechanics, threat actor attribution, or a finished patch at this stage.

Trap: saving a draft on the platform does not count as submitting. The clock runs against submission.

Clock 2

Full notification, within 72 hours

A fuller notification with the information available, including the nature of the vulnerability or incident and any corrective or mitigating measures taken or planned.

Also due around here: inform impacted users, and where appropriate all users, along with measures they can take.

Clock 3, and it splits in two

Final report
  • Actively exploited vulnerability: no later than 14 days after a corrective or mitigating measure is available
  • Severe incident: within one month of the 72 hour notification

The final report closes the case opened by the earlier notifications, covering root cause, mitigations applied, and impact.

Two points people miss

Reporting reaches products already on the market. A product shipped years ago sits inside this obligation from 11 September 2026, even though the essential requirements do not apply to it unless it undergoes a substantial modification.

Third-party components count. A vulnerability originating in a component you ship is reportable when it is actively exploited in your product. Commission guidance carves out cases where the vulnerable code is not exploitable in your product, for example because it is not reachable. That determination depends on component-level knowledge, which is where an accurate SBOM earns its keep.

Readiness test

If a reportable event happened tonight, could you submit tomorrow? You need a named accountable owner, platform registration and verified accounts, a known CSIRT coordinator, an escalation path that runs on nights and weekends, and a decision rule for "actively exploited" and "severe."

08

Penalties and enforcement

Market surveillance authorities can restrict or withdraw products. Fines are tiered.

National market surveillance authorities enforce the CRA. They can require corrective action, restrict or prohibit making a product available, and order withdrawal or recall. Article 64 sets maximum administrative fines in three tiers.

Maximum administrative fines

Essential requirements in Annex I, and manufacturer obligations in Articles 13 and 14EUR 15M

Or 2.5% of total worldwide annual turnover for the preceding financial year, whichever is higher

Other obligations under the regulationEUR 10M

Or 2% of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher

Incorrect, incomplete, or misleading information to notified bodies or authoritiesEUR 5M

Or 1% of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher

Authorities must consider the nature, gravity, and duration of the infringement, along with the size and market share of the operator. The regulation directs Member States to take account of the interests of small and medium-sized enterprises, including microenterprises. Fines do not apply to non-commercial open-source developers.

The realistic near-term exposure

For most organizations the first enforcement risk is not a fine. It is a missed reporting deadline, or a product held out of the EU market because conformity evidence is not ready.

A practical first 90 days

Suggested sequencing for a team starting from zero. Guidance, not a legal requirement.

Three phases

Tap a phase

Days 1 to 30

Decide what applies, then get reportable
  • Build the product inventory and determine scope and classification per product, writing down the reasoning
  • Name one accountable owner for the CRA program
  • Register on the ENISA Single Reporting Platform and verify the account
  • Identify your CSIRT coordinator and appoint an authorized representative if you are outside the EU
  • Write the 24 hour escalation path, including nights and weekends

Days 30 to 60

Prove your components, publish the front door
  • Produce SBOMs from the build pipeline rather than by hand
  • Establish how you learn that a component vulnerability is being actively exploited
  • Stand up a coordinated vulnerability disclosure policy and a security contact
  • Create an advisory location where users can find your notices

Days 60 to 90

Rehearse, then start the evidence file
  • Run a tabletop against the 24 and 72 hour clocks using a realistic third-party component scenario
  • Fix what the exercise breaks
  • Open the Annex VII technical documentation set per product line
  • Set the support period for each product
  • Begin notified body engagement if your classification requires one
