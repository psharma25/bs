Vendor risk, five generations

Every generation of vendor assessment solved the last one's problem

Third-party risk has been rebuilt roughly every five years, and each rebuild was a reasonable answer to how the previous method failed. Questionnaires answered the need for a record. Ratings answered staleness. Detection answered speed. Subprocessor mapping answered depth.

Each also carried an assumption that eventually stopped holding. This is a walk through the five generations, what each one solved, what broke it, and what the AI era asks for that none of them deliver.

Nothing here ended. Every method is still running, which is why programs feel heavy — most teams operate five instruments at once, having been resourced for one. Note also that the interval between generations is shortening: ten years to the second, seven to the third, then three, then one.

It was never really about the answers. It was about having asked.

What it solved

Before the questionnaire there was no record that diligence had happened at all. Standardised instruments — the SIG, the CAIQ, later industry variants — gave procurement a gate, gave auditors an artifact, and gave two companies a shared vocabulary for a conversation that had previously been ad hoc.

Crucially, it is still the only instrument that can ask about things you cannot observe from the outside: retention periods, personnel screening, what happens to data after termination, whether anyone is training on your records.

The assumption underneath

That the vendor's answer was accurate when written, and would remain accurate until the next review.

What broke it

Scale and cadence. A mid-size company went from forty vendors to four hundred, and a control that runs once a year against a self-reported answer degrades into an administrative ritual. Nobody was lying. The document was simply describing a company that no longer existed.

What carried forward

The questionnaire never went away and shouldn't. It remains the only route to unobservable facts. What changed is its status: from the assessment itself to one input among several.

The insight was that you could learn something true about a vendor without their cooperation.

What it solved

Staleness. Outside-in scanning — certificate hygiene, exposed services, patching cadence, botnet signals — produced a daily signal where previously there had been an annual document. It also made vendor risk legible to executives for the first time, because a letter grade travels in a way a 300-row spreadsheet does not.

The assumption underneath

That observable external hygiene is a reliable proxy for internal control quality.

What broke it

The proxy holds for some things and not others. A rating measures whether a company patches and configures its perimeter well. It says nothing about what that company does with your data once it arrives. A vendor can hold an excellent score and still train models on your customer records, retain them indefinitely, and route them through a provider you have never assessed.

What carried forward

Independence. The principle that the most reliable signals are the ones you gather yourself, rather than the ones the vendor hands you, is the most important idea in this lineage — and it becomes decisive in generation five.

SolarWinds, Log4Shell and MOVEit made one thing obvious: the question was no longer whether a vendor was well run, but how fast you could tell you were affected.

What it solved

Time to know. Breach telemetry, dark web monitoring, exploit intelligence mapped against your vendor list, and managed services that chase remediation on your behalf. The posture shifted from producing reports to running an operation — who is affected, what do we do today, who calls the vendor.

The assumption underneath

That the risk event is a compromise: something is exploited, breached or leaked, and there is an incident to respond to.

What broke it

In the AI era the significant events are frequently not compromises at all. A vendor ships a feature. A term changes. A new model provider appears in the processing chain. Nothing was attacked and nothing leaked — and yet your exposure changed materially. Detection tuned for compromise sees none of it.

What carried forward

The operational muscle. Named owners, defined SLAs, a runbook rather than a report. Generation five borrows this wholesale and simply points it at a different class of event.

Twenty vendors on the register. Three companies actually holding the data.

What it solved

Depth. Regulatory pressure — DORA in financial services, NIS2 across critical sectors, sector rules elsewhere — forced the question of what sits underneath each direct vendor. Discovery platforms began identifying vendor dependencies without relying on the vendor to disclose them, and concentration analysis surfaced the case where a single fourth-party failure propagates across many suppliers at once.

The assumption underneath

That the dependency chain can be reconstructed from published documentation plus external observation.

What broke it

Two limits, both material. The documentation half is unreliable: research published in May 2026 found that 63.6% of vendors advertising AI capabilities do not disclose a third-party AI subprocessor anywhere in their legal documentation. And the observation half is company-level, not tenant-level — it can tell you a vendor has an AI dependency, not whether your instance routes data to it.

What carried forward

The dependency register, concentration analysis, and — underrated — the contract mechanics. Notice clauses and objection rights only became interesting once anyone was reading them.

Third-party risk was built to answer who did we give data to. The question now is what are they doing with it this week.

What is actually different

Three things, and it is worth separating them because they have different owners.

  • Pace. In May 2026 Microsoft moved AI subprocessor notice to thirty days against a six-month standard, with the ability to disable that subprocessor running at least six months from notice. Reasonable commercially — and it means protection changed from automatic to something you have to actively use.
  • Control surface. The decision that matters most is a tenant setting owned by a business administrator, not a clause. It can revert after a release.
  • Distribution. Roughly half the consequence lands outside security: employment law in hiring tools, consent exposure in recording tools, change control in regulated workflows, evidence integrity in audit.

The shape of the problem

Vendor data processing has become dynamic, while third-party assurance is still point-in-time. We are governing a moving system with a static instrument — and the instrument is not wrong, it is simply measuring at the wrong frequency.

What the market solves, and what it doesn't

Every category in this lineage is now marketing an AI capability, and some of it is real. The honest read is that vendors cover discovery and evidence well, and cover configuration, contract mechanics and accountability not at all.

Assessment→Monitoring

ProblemVendors change how they process your data between reviews. The assessment expires and nobody is told.

MarketCompliance automation platforms monitor vendors continuously and trigger on breach signals.

GapThey read what vendors publish — and 63.6% of AI vendors never disclose their AI subprocessors.

YouOwn the trigger list and its SLAs. Diff DPAs, subprocessor pages and release notes yourself.

Contract→Configuration

ProblemThe control that matters is a tenant toggle owned by a business admin, and it reverts on release.

MarketSaaS posture tools track configuration drift. Data governance suites surface oversharing.

GapNo product ships an AI-specific settings baseline across the platforms you actually run.

YouWrite the baseline per platform. Verify by API quarterly and after every major release.

Third party→Fourth party

ProblemTwenty vendors on the register, three model providers actually holding the data.

MarketSecurity ratings platforms detect vendor AI dependencies without vendor self-disclosure.

GapCompany-level, not tenant-level. Neither touches your notice clauses or objection rights.

YouPut a monitored address on every notice clause. Calendar the objection deadline on arrival.

Security risk→Distributed risk

ProblemHalf of this lands in employment law, quality, audit and resilience — not in security.

MarketNo product category. AI management standards give the framing; GRC platforms sell readiness.

GapAccountability cannot be purchased. No platform assigns an owner or holds them to it.

YouWrite the routing table, name the owner per category, run a 30-minute quarterly forum.

Gatekeeper→Paver

ProblemProhibition relocates the risk to the employee tier, where there is no contract at all.

MarketShadow SaaS discovery tools find unsanctioned use and nudge people toward approved options.

GapDiscovery is not adoption. No tool makes your own approval process fast enough to use.

YouPublish three sanctioned tools bought at the right tier. Commit to a five-day intake.

What needs to be done

Five mechanisms. Each needs a name, an owner, a cadence and an artifact, or it is an intention rather than a control.

A vendor AI change watch

Daily diffs on subprocessor pages, DPAs and release notes, feeding five defined triggers — new AI feature, new AI subprocessor, region change, DPA amendment, acquisition — each with an SLA and a disposition.

Owner: TPRM · Artifact: trigger log · Evidence for SOC 2 CC3.2 and CC9.2

An AI configuration baseline

One page per major platform: AI features, training and product-improvement flags, retention, residency, connector allowlist, admin consent. Verified by API quarterly and after every major release, because that is when defaults return.

Owner: platform owner in IT · Artifact: signed baseline and drift log

Notice readiness

A monitored distribution list on every material notice clause, and an objection calendar opened the day notice arrives. An objection right with a thirty-day window and no named owner is functionally absent.

Owner: vendor management, with legal on clause language · Artifact: notice register

A routing table

Written agreement on who owns the consequence: HR for hiring tools, legal for consent and privilege, quality for anything touching a regulated process, finance and internal audit for approval automation. Security owns detection and the register, and explicitly not the others' decisions. Thirty minutes, quarterly, with the trigger and drift logs as standing inputs.

Owner: whoever chairs risk today · Artifact: routing table and minutes

A sanctioned catalog with a fast lane

Three entries on day one — one assistant, one notetaker, one coding tool — bought at the tier where your data is not used for training and configured to the baseline. Then a five-day intake commitment for anything else. The commitment is the product.

Owner: IT, security reviewing · Measure: shadow discovery falling while sanctioned adoption rises

Sequencing

  • Days 1–30. Discovery only, no controls. OAuth grants, conferencing bot-join logs, browser extension inventory, card and AP spend. Reconcile against the contract register; the gap between the two is your business case.
  • Days 30–60. Change watch running. Baselines written for the top three platforms. Notice addresses corrected.
  • Days 60–90. Routing table agreed in a forum. Catalog live with a published intake SLA.

Report two numbers upward and no more: the share of AI-touching vendors with a current configuration baseline, and the median days from vendor notice to a recorded decision. Both move, both are defensible, and neither can be improved by writing more policy.

The pattern worth noticing

In almost every documented case the vendor disclosed. It was in the terms, on a policy page, in a release note, or in one paragraph of a forty-page addendum. Nobody was deceived — the notice went to an alias created during onboarding three years ago, or to a page nobody diffs.

Which makes this a monitoring failure on our side rather than bad faith on theirs. A less satisfying story, and the only version that produces a fix.
