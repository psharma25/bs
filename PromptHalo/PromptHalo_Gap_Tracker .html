POL-IRP incident response coverage

POL-BCP business continuity coverage

Annex A charters CC9.1 here. Eight controls relevant, seven primary.

The last open finding in the library closes here. CC9.1-04 wanted a named alternate for every critical responsibility, with the Security Officer having one who holds equivalent access. The policy says plainly that this is the CEO, through break-glass access.

Written around people, not data centres. Infrastructure is in code and rebuilds; the part that needed writing down is what happens when the one person who knows something is unavailable. Where no genuine alternate exists, the concentration is recorded as a risk — an unrecorded single point of failure is the finding, a recorded one is a position.

The missed-test clause matters more now. With every cadence annual, a scheduled test not performed on time is the whole control rather than a delay, so it is recorded as a deficiency and escalated. That clause protects every other annual cadence in the library.

POL-AM asset management coverage

Eleven controls name POL-AM; four are primary to it. Annex A charters no criterion to asset management directly, which is why its controls arrive through CC2.1, CC5.2, CC6.1, CC6.5, CC6.8, CC7.1 and CC9.2 rather than a single criterion.

All eleven read Yes, and v1.2 made the basis honest. The policy now states plainly that the Company runs no device management platform, no endpoint detection software and no agent of any kind on personal devices, and does not intend to. Nothing in it requires a person to install Company software on a device they own or to permit inspection of it.

What replaced those claims. The inventory comes from provider records that already exist. The device list is a couple of columns on the personnel list, supplied by each person — make and operating system is enough. Device protections are the ones the operating system already offers, self-confirmed once a year in the acknowledgment everyone already signs. No agent, scan or inventory tool is involved.

One control is scoped rather than met in full. CC6.8-01 expects endpoint detection on every device. The policy does not claim that, and says instead that where a client contract requires managed endpoints the Company provides equipment — or declines the requirement rather than accepting it and leaving it unmet. Record that scoping in the plan of action with AM-A4 as the compensating control.

POL-HRS human resources security coverage

Annex A charters CC1.4 — commitment to competence — to HR Security, and a further set of controls name it. Fourteen controls are relevant: six where POL-HRS is the primary owner, five shared, and three chartered to CC1.4 but operated elsewhere.

All fourteen read Yes. Four were open before v1.1 — role competencies, contractors held to the same standard, performance review covering control responsibilities, and the screening record. POL-HRS now carries all four.

The design point is that contractors are the ordinary case, not the exception. Almost all personnel here are fractional or contract, so the policy places them in the same compliance-platform group as employees and lets them inherit the same policy, training and screening set automatically. A separate contractor tracker is what drifts, and the policy says so.

One dependency remains. Two controls point at STD-SCR-001, the screening standard, which is still absent from the library. Half a page, and it clears findings here and in POL-AC-001 together.

v1.2 rescoped the policy to the Company as it is. The offboarding trigger moved from a human resources system the Company does not run to the personnel list it does. Manager notification became whoever knows, because there is no management layer. The performance review became one dated CEO note covering everyone. Screening beyond identity and right to work is set by tier rather than assumed, and a contracting firm's written confirmation is accepted in place of repeating the check.

v1.3 removed cost and duplication. Two clauses now govern how the rest is read: requirements apply only so far as lawful and customary where each person works, and where a check, record or tool is called for, the least costly option that satisfies it is the one to use — no HR platform, screening subscription or learning management system is required. The contractor section went from nine clauses to seven, dropping a mandate to use a particular compliance platform, and Termination went from four to two because it was restating the offboarding process rather than adding to it.

POL-AUP acceptable use coverage

Annex A charters no criterion to Acceptable Use, which is why the control plan showed it owning nothing — a mapping defect rather than a redundant policy. v1.1 names the ten controls whose user-facing rules it states, so the document and the plan now agree.

Written for how the Company actually works. People are remote, on their own laptops, in Google Workspace, AWS, GitHub and AI tools. Nothing in the Policy requires software on a personal device, and it says plainly that the Company does not monitor them.

Five rules carry the risk. Work in Company accounts; keep client information in the cloud services rather than on laptops; never share or embed credentials; keep multi-factor on; report anything odd the same day. Everything after that is judgement, which is what keeps the Policy usable as the team grows.

One paid tool. A password manager, roughly $80 to $160 a month at ten people. The rest — OAuth approval on Workspace and GitHub, the AI register, the monitoring disclosure — costs nothing.

POL-LOG logging and monitoring coverage

Annex A charters CC7.2 to this policy. Nine controls are relevant, seven of them primary — the highest concentration of owned controls outside POL-ISP and POL-RSK.

Switch it on once, then let it run. AWS CloudTrail, Google Workspace audit logs, the GitHub organisation log and the product's own records cover almost everything that matters, and all four are native. Where a native feature and a product both exist, the policy takes the native one.

Five alerts, not fifty. Root or org-level account use, logging configuration changes, new admin grants, unapproved OAuth grants, secret-scanning findings — routed to a shared address so the record survives a departure. A long list that gets muted is worse than a short one that gets read.

The cadence is the risk here, not the substance. Twelve monthly disposition reviews is what a Type II sample needs, nil months included, and a missed month cannot be recreated. This is the control most likely to fail on timing rather than design.

Nothing reaches personal laptops. No logging agent, nothing collected. Because work happens in the cloud services, the account-side record is sufficient.

STD-SCR-001 screening standard coverage

The Screening Standard carries no criterion of its own — it exists because POL-HRS-001 and POL-AC-001 both refer to “the tier applied” and needed that to point at something definite. Six controls depend on it.

One tier, and it stays that way to 50 people. Identity and right to work, confirmed from documents viewed rather than stored. No criminal record check, credit check, adverse media search or paid screening service unless a client contract expressly requires it.

An NDA is not a substitute. A confidentiality agreement records what someone has undertaken; screening records who they are. The agreement is required separately under POL-HRS-001, and the Standard says so rather than conflating the two.

Tooling — consolidated recommendation

One view of what produces every artifact in the library, rather than a tool named separately in each policy. The position is deliberately conservative: the Company already pays for AWS, Google Workspace and GitHub, and almost everything below comes out of that stack or a spreadsheet.

Two paid additions are genuinely load-bearing. A password manager, without which POL-PWD and POL-AUP describe a control with nowhere to perform it, and phishing-resistant factors for administrative access. Together roughly $80–160 a month plus a one-off key cost.

A compliance platform buys calendar discipline, not controls. Every artifact in the table below can be produced without one. It is worth the money if the annual cadences would otherwise slip, or if a customer expects a trust page — not because any control requires it.

Six registers, one place. The risk register, vendor register, asset inventory, AI tool register, disposal register and retention schedule are all sheets. Six tools for six registers is how a ten-person programme becomes unmaintainable.

Assessment scale

The same 0 to 4 scale used in the Control Plan workbook. Set a score on any finding from the row of buttons beneath it. Scores are stored per snapshot date, so the baseline keeps its own.

Where the programme actually sits. Across all 164 controls the library holds 137 at score 1 and one at score 2. Nothing has reached 3. Drafting is close to done; the evidence clock has not started.

The step that costs real time is 1 to 3, not 0 to 1. Writing a clause takes an hour. Producing dated evidence for every occurrence across an observation period takes the period. annual controls need four occurrences and monthly ones twelve, so a control still at 1 in November cannot reach 3 for a December year-end however much effort is applied. Score 2 is the trap: the activity happens, nobody keeps the record, and it tests as a failure rather than as partial credit.
