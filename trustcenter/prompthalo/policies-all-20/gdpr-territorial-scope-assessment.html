GDPR and UK GDPR Territorial Scope Assessment

Whether EU and UK data protection law applies to PromptHalo Technologies, and what follows if it does

About this copy. This is an internal assessment record (version 1.0). Owner: Chief Executive Officer – Accountable Party. Prepared by: Security Officer. It records a scoping decision and the reasoning behind it. It is not legal advice and has not been reviewed by counsel.

1 Decision

The Company has assessed that neither the EU General Data Protection Regulation nor the UK GDPR currently applies to its processing, because the territorial scope tests in Article 3 are not met. On that basis the Company has not appointed a representative under Article 27, and does not publish one.

This decision is recorded here and in the risk register alongside the existing decision to scope the Privacy category of the Trust Services Criteria out of the current examination. It is reassessed annually and on any trigger in section 7.

This is a dated position, not a settled one. The Company intends to serve customers in the European Union and the United Kingdom as soon as it is commercially able. This assessment is therefore written to be superseded, and the sections that matter most are 7a and 7b: when the tests are met, and how the obligations are met without adding staff. The Company does not currently have capacity for additional headcount, and nothing in the expansion plan requires any.

2 Why This Assessment Exists

Company size does not create an exemption from Article 27. There is no small-business carve-out in the Regulation. The only exemption in Article 27(2) is narrow and conjunctive – processing must be occasional, must not include large-scale special category or criminal offence data, and must be unlikely to result in a risk to rights and freedoms – and the European Data Protection Board reads “occasional” as processing outside the regular course of business. The Company does not rely on that exemption.

The exemption the Company relies on sits one gate earlier: the Regulation does not reach it at all. That position must be documented, because the absence of a named representative in a published privacy notice is otherwise indistinguishable from a compliance failure.

3 Article 3(1) Establishment

Article 3(1) applies the Regulation to processing in the context of the activities of an establishment in the Union, regardless of where the processing occurs.

The Company is incorporated and operates in the United States, at 6475 Preston Rd, Unit 140, Frisco, TX 75034. It has no office, branch, subsidiary, agent or personnel in any Member State or in the United Kingdom. Article 3(1) is not engaged.

4 Article 3(2)(a) Offering Goods or Services

Article 3(2)(a) reaches a controller outside the Union where processing relates to offering goods or services to data subjects in the Union, whether or not payment is required. EDPB Guidelines 3/2018 are explicit that a website merely being accessible from the Union is not sufficient. What matters is evidence of an intention to target.

5 Article 3(2)(b) Monitoring Behaviour

Article 3(2)(b) reaches processing related to monitoring the behaviour of data subjects so far as that behaviour takes place within the Union. The EDPB treats tracking on the internet – including analytics, profiling and behavioural advertising – as monitoring for this purpose.

The Company sets strictly necessary cookies only. It runs no analytics cookies, no third-party analytics tool, no advertising or retargeting pixels, no session replay, no cross-site tracking and no fingerprinting. This is recorded and published in Cookie Policy and reflected in Privacy Notice.

Service reliability is measured from server-side request records, which are generated by the act of serving a request rather than by observing behaviour over time, and are retained for up to thirteen months under. The Company does not profile visitors, does not build behavioural segments, and does not combine request records with any other source to characterise an individual.

Article 3(2)(b) is not engaged. This is the load-bearing conclusion in this assessment, and it depends entirely on the no-tracking decision remaining true.

6 Position as a Processor

Where the Company processes personal data on a customer’s instructions, that data may in principle include individuals located in the Union or the United Kingdom. This does not by itself bring the Company within Article 3(2). The targeting or monitoring must be the Company’s own; a processor is not caught merely because its controller’s data subjects are in Europe.

What binds the Company in that situation is the customer’s data processing agreement under Article 28, not the Regulation acting on the Company directly. The obligations are comparable in practice – documented instructions, sub-processor notice, assistance with data subject requests, breach notification to the controller – and Privacy Policy already commits to them.

The Company does not currently have a data processing addendum of its own. That is recorded as a gap in the risk register.

6a Voluntary Standard and the Public Position

Two questions are kept apart in every artifact, and must stay apart.

  1. Does the Regulation apply? A legal question, answered by Article 3. Section 1 records the answer: not currently. Only this answer governs whether an Article 27 representative is required.

  2. What standard does the Company hold itself to? A policy choice. The Company extends the rights in Privacy Notice – access, correction, deletion, restriction, portability and objection – to everyone who asks, wherever they live, and uses Standard Contractual Clauses where a transfer requires a safeguard or a customer contract asks for one.

Meeting a standard voluntarily does not make the Company subject to it. Article 27 is triggered by Article 3, not by a published commitment. Conversely, a published commitment can be enforced contractually by a customer even where a supervisory authority could not act, so the Company states only what it will do.

The public artifacts are aligned to this position and must be changed together:

  • Privacy Notice, section Which law applies – states the applicability position, the absence of a representative, and the commitment to appoint one before serving UK or EEA customers.

  • Compliance ledger in index.html – the GDPR row reads Territorial scope assessed annually; GDPR-grade rights extended to everyone.

  • Trust Center FAQ – the GDPR entry gives the same two-part answer.

If any one of these is edited, the other two are edited in the same release. A public claim of applicability sitting beside an internal record denying it is the specific failure this section exists to prevent: in a dispute the internal document is discoverable, and the mismatch reads as knowing the public page overstated the position.

7a Expansion into the UK or the EEA

Expansion is intended, so the question is not whether these obligations arrive but when, and the answer is earlier than most companies assume.

The line is crossed by marketing, not by signature. Article 3(2)(a) reaches processing related to offering goods or services to data subjects in the Union. The EDPB reads that as evidenced intent to target, not as a completed sale. Directing outbound at European prospects, running a campaign that targets those markets, pricing in euro or sterling, or holding European prospect contacts in a CRM for the purpose of selling to them are all capable of engaging it. By the time a contract is signed the Regulation has usually applied for months.

That timing matters because an Article 27 representative appointed after processing has begun does not cure the period before it. Appointing one first is unremarkable; appointing one late is a breach that has already happened and is visible on the face of the record.

Sequence.

  1. Decide the go date before the first European outbound. Not the first contract, not the first qualified prospect – the first deliberate act of targeting.

  2. Appoint the representatives. EU Article 27 and UK Article 27 are separate appointments. Both are commercial subscriptions, not employees (section 7b).

  3. Name them in under the Article 13(1)(a) disclosure. An unnamed representative does not satisfy the obligation.

  4. Complete the record of processing activities (Article 30). It is a table, and the Company already holds most of its content across, and the sub-processor list.

  5. Publish the data processing addendum and the sub-processor list. A European customer asks for both in the first security review, and Article 28 requires the authorisation, notice period and objection right to live somewhere.

  6. Confirm the transfer mechanism. The Standard Contractual Clauses and UK Addendum are already cited in; a transfer impact assessment is recorded where one is needed.

  7. Reissue, the compliance ledger row and the FAQ in the same release, per the maintenance rule in section 6a. Nothing needs retracting – the current wording is a dated assessment, so moving from it is an update.

Recommendation. Because appointment is cheap, quick and reversible, while late appointment is not curable, the Company should appoint both representatives at the point European outbound begins rather than waiting for the analysis to be confirmed by a signed contract. The cost of being early is a small annual subscription. The cost of being late is a documented compliance failure during the exact period a European customer is running diligence.

7b Meeting the Obligations Without Additional Staff

The Company has no capacity for additional headcount. None of the following requires any.

The representative is not an employee. Article 27 requires a person or entity established in a Member State, appointed in writing, acting as a contact point for supervisory authorities and data subjects. It is supplied as an annual subscription by specialist providers – Prighter, DataRep, EDPO and Rickert among them – typically for a low three-figure sum per year per jurisdiction, with the UK appointment usually bundled. The provider holds a copy of the record of processing activities and forwards correspondence. Verify current pricing and terms before appointing.

A Data Protection Officer is almost certainly not required. Article 37 mandates one only where the organisation is a public authority, where core activities require regular and systematic monitoring of data subjects on a large scale, or where core activities involve large-scale processing of special category or criminal offence data. None applies: the Company runs no behavioural tracking, processes no special category data, and its core activity is security testing rather than monitoring individuals. already states there is no statutory DPO and that the Security Officer is accountable for privacy. That position survives expansion. The representative may not double as the DPO in any case – the EDPB treats the roles as incompatible.

What cannot be outsourced, and what it actually costs.

  • Record of processing activities – a spreadsheet, built once and reviewed annually. A day of work, not a role.

  • Data subject requests – answered within one month, extendable by two where a request is complex. At the Company’s volume this is a handful of requests a year, absorbed by the Security Officer under the existing process in.

  • Breach notification within 72 hours to the supervisory authority where a breach is notifiable. This is the one genuine operational commitment, because 72 hours is short for a small team and the clock starts at awareness rather than at confirmation. already sets a notification matrix; it needs a 72-hour row added and a named deputy so the obligation does not depend on one person being reachable.

  • Sub-processor change notice – a mailing list and a stated notice period, once the list exists.

The honest summary: the recurring cost of European expansion is a small subscription and roughly a day a quarter of the Security Officer’s time. The one obligation that is genuinely demanding for a team this size is the 72-hour breach notification clock, and it is met by preparation rather than by people – a drafted notification template, a named deputy, and a decision rule for when the clock starts. That preparation should be done before expansion, not during an incident.

Deferred, not avoided. If the Company later grows into regular large-scale monitoring, or begins processing special category data on behalf of European customers, the Article 37 analysis is rerun. Nothing in this section is permanent.

8 What to Do if a Trigger Fires

  1. Reopen this assessment and record the changed facts before the activity starts.

  2. Obtain legal advice on whether Article 3(2) is engaged. Do not appoint a representative pre-emptively – naming one is itself an assertion that the Regulation applies.

  3. If it is engaged, identify the Member State where the relevant data subjects are and appoint a representative established there, by written mandate. A separate appointment is required for the United Kingdom under UK GDPR Article 27.

  4. Publish the representative’s name and contact details in Privacy Notice under the Article 13(1)(a) disclosure. An unnamed representative does not satisfy the obligation.

  5. Provide the representative with a copy of the record of processing activities, and define how data subject requests and supervisory authority correspondence route to the Security Officer.

  6. The representative may not also act as data protection officer; the EDPB treats the roles as incompatible.

  7. Review for statements that now create obligations the Company must meet, and reissue it.

9 Limits of This Assessment

This record is a documented business judgement, not a legal opinion, and it has not been reviewed by counsel. It should be before the Company signs a contract that turns on it.

Two points are worth noting even while the conclusion holds. First, the earlier inconsistency between this assessment, Privacy Notice and the compliance ledger has been resolved: all three now state the same two-part position, described in section 6a. They are to be maintained together.

Second, the records of processing exemption in Article 30(5) is not the same exemption and does not follow from this one. It applies below 250 employees but falls away where processing is regular, which personnel data is. The Company maintains a record of processing activities as good practice regardless of whether one is required.
