GuideHow to compute App Risk
App Risk = 25 × (0.40 × Data + 0.35 × Exposure + 0.25 × Scale) range 25 to 100
ValueData (40%)Exposure (35%)Scale (25%): people affected
1Public or no personal data. Marketing content, docs.Internal only. No inbound route from outside the network.Under 1,000 records or people
2Internal business data, employee names and work contact details.Internal through VPN or SSO, or a partner IP allowlist.1,000 to 100,000
3PII: names with date of birth, home addresses, personal emails, chat transcripts.Internet, but an invited, contracted, or SSO account is required.100,000 to 1 million
4Regulated: PHI, payment card data, SSNs, credentials or keys at scale.Internet, anonymous or open self-registration.Over 1 million

How to select each value

  • Data: pick the most sensitive data the app stores, processes, or can reach with its own credentials. Include logs, backups, and anything its cloud role can read. For an AI agent, include data reachable through its tools.
  • Exposure: pick the most open path into the app, including admin panels, APIs, and webhooks. One public endpoint makes the whole app public unless it is network-separated. Open self-registration counts as 4.
  • Scale: count the people who would be harmed by a breach: unique residents, members, or applicants whose records the app holds or can reach. Do not count staff users or servers. If you only have an estimate, round up to the next band.
  • Tie-breaker: when torn between two values, choose the higher one and write down why.
  • Review: re-score yearly, and whenever the app adds a new data type, a new public endpoint, or grows tenfold.

Reasoning

  • Why Data carries 40%: it decides breach impact, notification duties, and regulatory exposure. A breach of SSNs or PHI costs far more than a breach of public content, regardless of how it happens.
  • Why Exposure carries 35%: it decides likelihood. Internet-facing systems are probed constantly; internal ones need a foothold first.
  • Why Scale carries 25%: it multiplies the damage but rarely changes whether an incident happens.
  • Why "most sensitive" and "most open": attackers pick the weakest door and the most valuable room. Averaging hides both.
  • Why round up when unsure: under-scoring an app means slower fixes and lighter testing; over-scoring costs only some extra scanning.
  • What the EOHHS table shows: almost every system lands in Tier 1 or 2 because nearly all of them hold regulated data. In an HHS portfolio, App Risk sets the floor, and exploitability does most of the ranking.

How to select

  • T: check in this order and stop at the first match: KEV, vendor and threat intel reports, public exploits, then EPSS.
  • R: start at 0.6. Move up or down only with evidence you can attach to the ticket.
  • X: use the easiest path an attacker could take, not the intended one.
  • CVSS: keep the base score as published. Do not lower it because the app is internal; App Risk and X already account for that. Only a verified compensating control that limits what an attacker can reach justifies a CVSS environmental score, and the control must be recorded.
  • With Mythos: T is raised to at least 0.7 when R is above 0. You do not select it; the page applies it.

Reasoning

  • Why multiply instead of add: an attack needs all three: a way to exploit (T), code that runs (R), and a way in (X). If any one is missing, the attack fails, so a near-zero factor should pull the whole score down.
  • Why check T in that order: confirmed exploitation is the strongest evidence; EPSS is a statistical estimate and the weakest. Use the strongest evidence you have.
  • Why R defaults to 0.6: scoring every unanalyzed finding at 1.0 floods the queue. Scoring it at 0.2 hides real risk. 0.6 keeps unknowns visible and rewards teams that do the analysis.
  • Why X uses the easiest path: attackers do not use your intended workflow. A forgotten admin route or an open signup page is the real front door.
  • Why the Mythos floor is 0.7: it treats every reachable bug as if a proof of concept were public. That matches a world where exploit writing is cheap, without assuming every bug is already weaponized (0.9) or exploited (1.0).

Rules for every priority

  • Missed SLA: the owner requests a risk acceptance with a compensating control and an expiry date. The Security Officer approves P0 and P1 exceptions.
  • Re-score on change: re-run the score when the vulnerability lands in KEV, a public exploit appears, or the app's exposure changes.
  • Which column to act on: use the "Without Mythos" priority by default. For Tier 1 apps, plan to the higher of the two columns.

Reasoning

  • Why 72 hours for P0: once a flaw is known to be exploited, attacks against exposed systems often begin within days. A deadline measured in weeks leaves the door open.
  • Why mitigate within 24 hours: a proper fix may need testing and a release window. A mitigation such as a WAF rule or disabling a feature closes most of the risk now and buys time for the real fix.
  • Why review logs on P0: if the flaw was exploitable before you found it, it may already have been used. Fixing it does not tell you whether data left.
  • Why rescan to close: a merged pull request is not proof. The same scanner or test that found the issue must confirm it is gone.
  • Why risk acceptance expires: threats change. An exception that made sense when no exploit existed may be wrong a month later.
  • Why Tier 1 plans to the Mythos column: those systems hold the most sensitive data at the largest scale. Planning ahead of the threat costs less than catching up after it arrives.

Should we use it?

Yes, as a testing checklist for each app type, not as a scoring input. The OWASP lists are awareness documents: they tell you where to look, not how risky a specific finding is.

How it connects to the score

  • Pick tests and scanner rules from the list that matches the app type. An API needs API Top 10 tests; a web scanner alone misses most of them.
  • Badge, do not score. Put the OWASP category under each finding (for example SSRF: A10:2021, API7:2023) and use it for reporting, coverage and trend analysis. Adding it to the score would double count the same weakness.
  • Coverage gaps are false negatives. If a Tier 1 app has never been tested against its list, record that gap in the coverage matrix.
  • Apps can be several types. DTA Connect is a web portal, a mobile app, and an API. Test against all three lists.

Legend: OWASP = Open Worldwide Application Security Project. A01, M1, API1, LLM01 = category numbers in each list. SSRF appears as API7 in the API list.

    Why this value

    KEV versus "in the wild"

    CISA KEV lists vulnerabilities with confirmed exploitation and a clear fix. It is reliable but it lags: vendors and threat intel teams often report exploitation days or weeks before a KEV entry appears. Treat a credible in-the-wild report the same as KEV. Both set T to 1.0.

    Anthropic reported in April 2026 that its restricted Claude Mythos Preview model found thousands of high-severity zero-days across major operating systems and browsers, and built a browser exploit that chained four vulnerabilities. Access is limited to Project Glasswing partners today.

The pipeline, redrawn

Each stage hands one consolidated output to the next. Scanners do not talk to the dashboard directly; everything goes through ASPM (stage 3), which is where duplicates collapse and noise is removed.

What changed from the original picture

One arrow per stage

Real pipelines pass a normalized batch between stages. Stage 2 emits SARIF and CycloneDX; stage 3 emits unique, validated findings with a VEX status; stage 4 adds an exploitability score.

Section 2

Resolving false positives and false negatives in ASPM

A false positive wastes engineering time and erodes trust in the program. A false negative is worse: it is risk nobody knows about. Handle them with two different mechanisms.

False positives: the scanner flagged something that is not a vulnerability

  1. Require a reason code and evidence. Use a fixed list: TEST_CODE, SANITIZED, NOT_IN_RANGE, NOT_SHIPPED, NOT_REACHABLE, DEAD_SECRET. Attach the proof (file path, call graph output, screenshot).
  2. Fix the rule, not the finding. If the same pattern recurs, add a path exclusion or rule tweak in the scanner config (for example, Semgrep paths.exclude: src/test/** for one rule). Clicking "ignore" 200 times is not tuning.
  3. Every suppression expires. Default 12 months. Expired suppressions reopen automatically for re-review.
  4. Reopen on code change. The fingerprint includes the file and sink. If that code changes, the suppression is invalidated.
  5. Second reviewer for Critical and High. A developer cannot close their own Critical as a false positive.
  6. Measure FP rate per rule. FP rate = closed-as-FP / total triaged. Tune any rule above 20%; disable rules above 50% that you cannot tune.

False negatives: a real vulnerability that no scanner reported

  1. Keep a coverage matrix. Rows are apps, columns are scanner types, cells show the last successful scan date. An empty or stale cell is a known blind spot.
  2. Log every escaped defect. Any issue found by a pentest, bug bounty, customer, or incident that no tool caught is an escape. Record which scanner should have found it and why it did not.
  3. Turn each escape into a control. Usually one of: a custom SAST rule, authenticated DAST, a new scanner type, or an exclusion that was too broad.
  4. Seed known vulnerabilities. Run scanners against a test app with planted bugs (OWASP Benchmark, Juice Shop, or your own). If a tool stops finding them, its config broke.
  5. Diff tools against each other. If DAST confirms an injection that SAST missed in the same code, SAST has a rule or language gap.
  6. Check SBOM completeness. Compare the SBOM to the lockfile and to libraries loaded at runtime. Vendored, shaded, or copied code is a classic SCA blind spot.
  7. Measure escape rate. Escapes / (escapes + tool-found true positives) per quarter. It should fall over time.

Section 3

Is the finding really valid?

For SCA, a scanner matching a package name and version to a CVE is the start of the analysis, not the end. Work down this ladder. Stop at the first "no", and record the answer as a VEX status.

  1. Is the package actually present?

    Check the lockfile, not the manifest. A package.json range or a Maven property can point at a version that is never resolved.

    No → VEX not_affected, justification component_not_present.

  2. Is the resolved version in the affected range?

    Use OSV.dev ranges, not string matching. For OS packages, check the distro tracker: Debian, Ubuntu, and Red Hat backport fixes without changing the upstream version number, so a "vulnerable" version string may already be patched.

    No → close as false positive, reason NOT_IN_RANGE.

  3. Is it shipped to production?

    Dev, test, and build-only dependencies (devDependencies, Maven test scope) do not run in production. Confirm against the container image SBOM.

    No → VEX not_affected, vulnerable_code_not_present. Still fix if it runs in CI with secrets.

  4. Is the vulnerable function reachable from your code?

    Use call-graph reachability: govulncheck for Go, Snyk or Semgrep Supply Chain reachability, Endor Labs, or a manual grep for the vulnerable API. Runtime agents can confirm whether the class is even loaded.

    No → VEX not_affected, vulnerable_code_not_in_execute_path. Reflection and plugins can fool static call graphs, so schedule the upgrade anyway at a lower priority.

  5. Can an attacker control the input?

    Many library CVEs need attacker-controlled data to reach the sink, or a non-default setting. Log4Shell needed user strings to reach a lookup. Text4Shell needs untrusted input passed to StringSubstitutor interpolation.

    No → VEX not_affected, vulnerable_code_cannot_be_controlled_by_adversary.

  6. Do compensating controls block it?

    A WAF rule, network isolation, or disabled feature lowers likelihood. It reduces exposure (factor X in section 6); it rarely closes the finding.

    Partial → VEX not_affected, inline_mitigations_already_exist only if the mitigation is in the code path itself.

Section 5

Application risk formula

Rank applications before you rank findings. Three inputs, each scored 1 to 4: data sensitivity (D), internet exposure (Eapp), and scale (S), meaning records or users at risk.

App Risk = 25 × ( 0.40·D + 0.35·E_app + 0.25·S ) range 25 to 100
ScoreD: data sensitivity (40%)Eapp: exposure (35%)S: scale (25%)
1Public or no personal dataInternal only, no inbound route< 1,000 records or users
2Internal business data, employee contact infoInternal through VPN or SSO, or partner allowlist1,000 to 100,000
3PII: names with DOB, addresses, emails, chat transcriptsInternet, invite-only or SSO account required100,000 to 1 million
4Regulated: PHI, payment card, SSNs, credentialsInternet, anonymous or open self-registration> 1 million

Why these weights: data sensitivity sets breach impact and regulatory exposure, so it leads. Exposure sets likelihood. Scale multiplies blast radius but rarely changes whether an incident happens. Tune the weights once a year, not per app.

Section 6

Exploitability analysis

CVSS tells you how bad a vulnerability could be. Exploitability tells you how likely it is to be used against this app. Three questions, multiplied, so any single "no" pulls the score down hard.

E = 10 × T × R × X range 0 to 10 T = threat evidence (is anyone exploiting it?) R = reachability (can it run in our code?) X = exposure (what does an attacker need first?)

T: threat evidence

1.0In CISA KEV, or exploitation seen in your logs
0.9Weaponized public exploit (Metasploit, Nuclei), or reproduced by your team
0.7Public proof of concept only
0.5EPSS ≥ 0.10, no public PoC; or first-party bug that is plausible but not reproduced
0.25EPSS 0.01 to 0.10; or pattern match only
0.1EPSS < 0.01, no PoC

R: reachability

1.0Confirmed: call graph hit, DAST reproduced, or live secret
0.6Unknown: no reachability analysis available
0.2Loaded, but vulnerable function not called or input not attacker controlled
0Not shipped: dev or test only. Close as VEX not_affected.

X: exposure and preconditions

1.0Internet, no auth or open self-signup, no user interaction
0.7Internet, invite-only or SSO account required
0.6Internet, victim must click or upload
0.4Internal network, VPN, or private repo access
0.2Local or admin access required

Where this comes from

Google does not publish a single internal scoring formula. This model combines the public building blocks that Google, FIRST, and CISA do publish and use:

FactorPublic sourceWhat it contributes
TCISA KEV catalog; FIRST EPSSKEV is confirmed exploitation. EPSS is the estimated probability of exploitation activity in the next 30 days.
RGoogle OSV.dev and OSV-Scanner; Go's govulncheckOSV gives precise affected version ranges. govulncheck reports a vulnerability only when your code calls the vulnerable function, which is the reachability idea.
XChromium security severity guidelinesChromium rates bugs by what an attacker must already have: user interaction, a compromised process, or a non-default setting. Each precondition lowers severity.
DecisionCISA SSVCA decision tree on exploitation status, automatability, and impact that ends in Track, Track*, Attend, or Act. The P bands in section 7 map to those outcomes.
Same CVE, two apps. Text4Shell on the Patient Portal (unreachable, R = 0.2) scores E = 1.8 and Priority 17.6 (P2). The same CVE on the Marketing site, if it passed form input into StringSubstitutor (R = 1.0), scores E = 9.0 and Priority 45 (P1) despite the lower App Risk of 51. Reachability moves priority more than tier does.
